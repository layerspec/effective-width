\author{Cheng-Chung~Li%

\markboth{IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence}%
{Li: Measuring the Effective Width of Convolutional Layers}

\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}
How much of a convolutional layer's channel width does a trained network
use? Two published depth profiles of this quantity disagree. We show that
the disagreement is largely about three choices the profile literature
makes without comment, the denominator, the covariance estimator and the
tensor read, each of which moves a profile by more than the effects
reported. Dividing by the attainable rank instead of the nominal width, a
bound that is known but has never been carried into a profile, removes an
apparent two-population structure in all 14 ImageNet models with
$1\!\times\!1$ expansion layers; estimating the covariance after global
pooling lowers the value on 687 of 703 dense layers; and the output of a
residual block exceeds the rank bound of its last convolution in 161 of 161
bottleneck blocks. The resulting protocol recovers the published CIFAR-10
profile of Garg \emph{et al.} ($r = 0.98$) and the ImageNet values of
Elmoznino and Bonner ($\rho = 0.997$). Because the pre-activation output
covariance is exactly $W\Sigma_{\rm patch}W^{\top}$, the effective width
separates into an architectural, a data and a kernel factor. Measured on 44
trained checkpoints of eleven families against their own initialisations,
training moves the bottleneck from the data to the kernel, which becomes a
matched filter for its input. The widths the protocol reads after the
nonlinearity can be trained to: a VGG-16 built at them matches full width on
CIFAR-10 at two thirds of the parameters, and a pre-registered control shows
that the unused width belongs to the few-class task and closes monotonically
with the number of classes. Orthogonality penalties realise half of the
width gain the decomposition predicts. Code, spectra and the pre-registered
analysis plan are released.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Convolutional networks, effective dimensionality, channel covariance,
network width, representation analysis.
\end{IEEEkeywords}}

\IEEEraisesectionheading{\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} number of channels in a convolutional layer is fixed
before training, by inheritance from a published architecture or by
search, and how many of them the trained layer uses is known, if at all,
only afterwards \cite{garg2019lowEffort,elmoznino2024highDim,lin2020hrank}.
Theory bounds the number without locating it: the infinite-width limit
describes the regime in which no feature is learned
\cite{jacot2018ntk,lee2020wide}, minimum-width theorems say what a family
\emph{can} represent \cite{park2021minimum}, and rank bounds bracket a
layer from above \cite{han2021rexnet,kim2018rank1} and below
\cite{daneshmand2020bn}. Every usable number is post hoc.

The post-hoc numbers disagree. Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort},
counting the principal components of the channel covariance that reach
$99.9\%$ of the variance on CIFAR VGGs, report a profile that saturates in
the middle of the network and collapses in its last layers; Elmoznino and
Bonner \cite{elmoznino2024highDim}, with the participation ratio over
\num{500}\,+ ImageNet layers, report it rising with depth. Setting out to
reconcile the two, we found that the measurements differ in three choices
made without comment, and that each choice moves a profile by more than the
effects either paper reports: what the count is divided by, whether spatial
positions or pooled images are the observations, and whether the
convolution output or the block output is read. A $1\!\times\!1$ expansion
from $C_{\rm in}$ to $C_{\rm out} > C_{\rm in}$ channels cannot have output
rank above $C_{\rm in}$, a bound stated before
\cite{han2021rexnet,kim2018rank1} but never carried into a profile; 20 of
ResNet-50's 53 convolutions are such layers, and a profile divided by
nominal width reads them as a tenth used whatever they have learned.

In this paper we fix a measurement protocol in which each of the three
choices is explicit and its consequence quantified, and we build it on one
identity: before its nonlinearity, a convolution's output covariance is
exactly $W\Sigma_{\rm patch}W^{\top}$. The identity splits the effective
width of a layer into what the architecture permits, the attainable rank
$r_{\max}$; what the data supplies, $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$; and what learning
does, the alignment of $W$ with $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$; and each factor comes
with a proposition, a bound and a measurement. We validate the protocol
against both published estimators at both tensors, measure 19 public
ImageNet checkpoints and then 44 trained checkpoints of eleven families
against their own initialisations under a pre-registered plan, and put the
ruler to use.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{What is measured.}\label{sec:four} Everything here is a functional of
the channel-covariance spectrum at one tensor: $k^*(\tau)$, participation
ratio, effective rank. None is a manifold intrinsic dimension, and the gap
is not small: for one VGG-16 layer Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} report a TwoNN dimension of about \num{18}
against about \num{200} principal components at $90\%$ variance. The linear
count is nonetheless the object that width is about, because the consumer
of a layer's output is the next convolution, linear in its input patch;
Section~\ref{sec:projection} makes that operational.

\textbf{Depth profiles of dimensionality.}\label{sec:profiles} The reported shape follows the
measure: manifold dimension rises then falls
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic,recanatesi2019dimensionality,cohen2020manifolds};
the pooled participation ratio rises \cite{elmoznino2024highDim}; the
position-sampled $k^*$ saturates and collapses on CIFAR
\cite{garg2019lowEffort}; the numerical rank of deep layers falls to about
the class count on CIFAR within the first few hundred steps, the
``tunnel'' \cite{masarczyk2023tunnel,harun2024tunnelVariables}, as neural
collapse predicts for the penultimate layer \cite{papyan2020neuralCollapse}.
Jacobian rank \cite{feng2022rankDiminishing} and the spatial rank of a
single feature map \cite{lin2020hrank} are different objects. The bound
$r_{\max}$ that Section~\ref{sec:stats} divides by is not new: output rank
cannot exceed input dimension for the softmax layer \cite{yang2018softmax},
for attention heads \cite{bhojanapalli2020lowRank} and for convolutional
expansion layers \cite{han2021rexnet,kim2018rank1}, where Han \emph{et al.}
turn it into a design rule from random-weight networks. No depth profile
measured on trained networks divides by it. The supplement tabulates what
each of these studies reads, samples, computes and normalises.

\textbf{Tensor, initialisation and the kernel.}\label{sec:where} Garg \emph{et al.}
and NORTH\textsuperscript{*} \cite{maile2022north} read the convolution
output; Ansuini \emph{et al.} and Elmoznino and Bonner read the block
output, after the shortcut and the nonlinearity, which Han \emph{et al.}
show can raise the rank above the expansion bound again. Whether a profile
is learned is left open: Elmoznino and Bonner note that architecture can
set effective dimensionality independently of learning and do not pursue
it; Ansuini \emph{et al.} report that the PC-ID profile of an untrained
VGG-16 is qualitatively the trained one (their Fig.~5C); Saxe \emph{et al.}
\cite{saxe2014exact} show for deep linear networks that the singular
structure of the representation is set by the input--output covariance and
the initialisation together, which makes the comparison with one's own
initialisation the natural control. Orthogonality regularisers
\cite{bansal2018orthogonality,huang2018own,wang2020ocnn} penalise a
kernel's distance from an isometry; an over-complete layer
($C_{\rm out} > C_{\rm in}k_hk_w$) cannot be orthogonal, and Massart
\cite{massart2022orthogonal} characterises what each penalty minimises
instead. Huang \emph{et al.} \cite{huang2018own,huang2020oni} prove that an
orthogonal kernel decorrelates its output only if its input is white. At
the tensor we read, the output covariance is $W\Sigma_{\rm patch}W^{\top}$:
the effective width is joint in the kernel this literature regularises and
the patch covariance it assumes white, and Section~\ref{sec:decompose}
measures the two.

\textbf{Statistics and sampling.} Convolutional spectra are power-law with
exponent below one \cite{ghosh2022powerLaws,kong2022v1Eigenspectrum}, so a
Marchenko--Pastur edge does not exist and we use variance-fraction and
entropy statistics with a threshold sensitivity. The rank estimators in use,
the participation ratio and its finite-sample correction
\cite{chun2026estimating}, effective rank \cite{roy2007effectiveRank},
RankMe \cite{garrido2023rankme} and the exponent $\alpha$
\cite{agrawal2022alphaReq}, weight head and tail differently and are
compared on identical layers in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}. Of the estimator
biases Pospisil and Pillow \cite{pospisil2025revisiting} list, sampling
noise and the $n$-eigenvalue ceiling apply here and Section~\ref{sec:samplesize}
bounds them.

\textbf{Width before training.} In the lazy regime the rank of every layer
stays at its initial value; feature learning moves it \cite{yang2021feature},
and once it does a narrower trained network predicts a wider one
\cite{vyas2023feature}. Training-free scores
\cite{mellor2021naswot,tanaka2020synflow,abdelfattah2021zero,chen2021tenas}
give one number per network and none estimates a layer's trained width.
Daneshmand \emph{et al.} \cite{daneshmand2020bn} prove that batch
normalisation keeps the rank at initialisation at least of order the square
root of the width and observe that SGD largely preserves the last layer's
rank. NORTH\textsuperscript{*} grows layers on a statistic of our family,
the $\epsilon$-rank of the post-activation matrix over the width, and does
not report it as a profile or against an untrained control. Redundancy in
trained convolutions is established
\cite{suau2020filterDistillation,waleffe2023pcn,nanda2023diffusedRedundancy};
none of that work reports a normalised depth profile. In contrast, we report the quantity as a profile across depth, on one scale for every architecture, against an untrained control.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A validated protocol.} We measure the channel covariance of
  the convolution output from sampled positions, at $n/C \ge 50$,
  normalised by attainable rank, with depthwise convolutions kept apart. The
  protocol recovers the 13-layer CIFAR-10 profile of \cite{garg2019lowEffort}
  at $r = 0.98$ and the block-output participation ratios of
  \cite{elmoznino2024highDim} at $\rho = 0.997$ (Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg}).

  \item \textbf{Three pitfalls, quantified.} On 19 checkpoints, normalising
  by attainable rank removes the bounded-versus-unbounded split in all 14
  models with expansion layers; the pooled estimator is lower on 687 of 703
  dense layers, by more than the profile's own adjacent-layer step in 18 of
  19 networks; and the block output exceeds the bound on its last
  convolution in 161 of 161 bottleneck blocks
  (Sections~\ref{sec:rmax}--\ref{sec:blockout}).

  \item \textbf{Trained versus initialised profiles.} Plain, basic-residual
  and dense families keep the architecture's layer ordering
  ($\rho = 0.6$--$0.75$ against initialisation) and gain a level; the six
  ImageNet ResNet-50 recipes agree with each other ($0.84$) and with
  initialisation not at all ($-0.08$). A controlled experiment registered
  before its data finds no block effect, so the reordering belongs to the
  data or the recipes. Nine trajectories put the ordering in place by epoch
  40--60 while accuracy is still at $84$--$88\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:learned}).

  \item \textbf{An exact decomposition.} Training moves the bottleneck from
  the data to the kernel: at initialisation $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ is
  low-rank and the kernel isotropic; after training $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ is
  full-rank, the layer ordering follows the kernels' Gram spectra, and the
  kernel is a matched filter for its input's principal directions
  ($\rho_{\rm align} \approx +0.9$, zero at initialisation). The
  decomposition predicts that orthogonalising a trained kernel adds about
  $0.3\,r_{\max}$; the SO and SRIP penalties, tested as registered, realise
  half of it (Section~\ref{sec:decompose}).

  \item \textbf{The measured widths can be trained to.} Truncating every
  layer to its $k^*(0.999)$ subspace at inference leaves accuracy unchanged
  in six of eight checkpoints, and $k^*(0.95)$ does not
  (Section~\ref{sec:projection}). A VGG-16 built from scratch at the
  post-activation $k^*(0.999)$ widths matches full width on CIFAR-10 at two
  thirds of the parameters; on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1k the same widths are
  the full ones, and a registered class-count control shows the unused
  width is the few-class task's, closing monotonically with the number of
  classes and appearing only once the task has been optimised long enough
  (Section~\ref{sec:a18}).
\end{enumerate}

The analysis was specified before the ImageNet data were collected. The
registered hypotheses concerned the \emph{shape} of the profile and none
survived a paired test over all layers; the results labelled
pre-registered below were each registered before their own data, and those
labelled exploratory were found in the data. The plan, its deviation log,
the code and the spectra are released (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The Measured Object}
\label{sec:object}

For a layer with output $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C \times H \times W}$
over $N$ images, the object is the $C \times C$ covariance of the channel
vector $y_{n,:,h,w}$ at one spatial position. The literature estimates it in
two ways that are not the same object. The \emph{position-sampled}
estimator draws $p$ positions per image and treats each as an observation,
$n = Np$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Sigma}^{\text{pos}}
  = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{n,(h,w) \in \mathcal{P}_n}
    \bigl(y_{n,:,h,w}-\bar{y}\bigr)\bigl(y_{n,:,h,w}-\bar{y}\bigr)^{\!\top}.
  \label{eq:covpos}
\end{equation}
The \emph{globally pooled} estimator averages each map over space and treats
each image as an observation, $n = N$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Sigma}^{\text{pool}}
  = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n}
    \bigl(\tilde{y}_n-\bar{\tilde{y}}\bigr)
    \bigl(\tilde{y}_n-\bar{\tilde{y}}\bigr)^{\!\top},
  \quad
  \tilde{y}_n = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} y_{n,:,h,w}.
  \label{eq:covpool}
\end{equation}
Pooling removes the within-image spatial variance rather than summarising
it. Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort} used the first and
Elmoznino and Bonner \cite{elmoznino2024highDim} the second, and the two
had not been compared on the same layers; we compute both. Under pooling
$n$ is the image count, so a $2048$-channel layer over $10^4$ images is
estimated at $n/C \approx 5$, which Section~\ref{sec:samplesize} shows is
not enough.

\subsection{Summary Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

With $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_C \ge 0$ the eigenvalues of the
estimate, we report
\begin{align}
  k^*(\tau) &= \min\Bigl\{k : \textstyle\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i
               \big/ \sum_i \lambda_i \ \ge\ \tau \Bigr\},
               \label{eq:kstar}\\
  \mathrm{PR} &= \Bigl(\textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\Bigr)^{\!2}
                 \Big/ \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i^2 ,
               \label{eq:pr}\\
  \mathrm{erank} &= \exp\Bigl(-\textstyle\sum_i p_i \log p_i\Bigr),
                    \quad p_i = \lambda_i \big/ \textstyle\sum_j \lambda_j ,
               \label{eq:erank}
\end{align}
the statistics of \cite{garg2019lowEffort}, of \cite{elmoznino2024highDim}
and of Roy and Vetterli \cite{roy2007effectiveRank}, the last responding to
the tail of the spectrum where PR does not. Each is divided by a width, and
which width is the point. A layer's output at one position is a linear map
of its receptive-field patch, so the rank of $\hat{\Sigma}$ is bounded by the
rank of that map and not by $C$ \cite{kim2018rank1,han2021rexnet}. For
$C_{\rm in}$ input channels, a $k_h \times k_w$ kernel and $g$ groups the
weight matrix is block-diagonal with disjoint supports, so
\begin{equation}
  r_{\max} = g \cdot \min\!\Bigl(
     \tfrac{C_{\rm in}}{g} k_h k_w,\ \tfrac{C_{\rm out}}{g}
  \Bigr).
  \label{eq:rmax}
\end{equation}
The bound holds at the convolution output, before the nonlinearity and
before a residual addition, either of which can raise the rank above it
\cite{han2021rexnet}. Where $r_{\max} < C$ the ratio $k^*/C$ cannot exceed
$r_{\max}/C$ whatever the layer has learned; a bottleneck's $1\!\times\!1$
expansion has $r_{\max}/C = 0.25$ by construction. We report $k^*/C$, the
registered statistic and the one comparable with prior work, and
$k^*/r_{\max}$ beside it. $k^*(0.95)/C$ is the registered primary outcome;
$\tau = 0.999$, the criterion of \cite{garg2019lowEffort}, is used where we
compare with their table and where function is at stake
(Section~\ref{sec:projection}).

\subsection{Estimation}

Covariances are accumulated in one pass with the pairwise update of Chan
\emph{et al.} \cite{chan1983variance} in double precision; single-precision
accumulation of uncentred products loses exactly the small eigenvalues that
$k^*(\tau)$ near $\tau = 1$ depends on. Positions are subsampled ($p = 16$)
rather than exhausted, since adjacent positions are correlated and $NHW$
would overstate the sample count on which the gate below rests; $n = Np$ is
the conservative count.

\subsection{Sample-Size Gate}
\label{sec:samplesize}

A covariance from $n < C$ observations is rank-deficient by construction,
and well above that point the small eigenvalues remain biased downward
\cite{pospisil2025revisiting,chun2026estimating}. The plan fixed, before the
data, that no row with $n/C < 50$ is reported and that every reported
statistic must have flattened in $n/C$. The number was a convention; it has
a derivation from Lawley's expansion \cite{lawley1956latentRoots}.

\begin{proposition}[finite-sample bias of the captured fraction]
\label{prop:gate}
Let $l_1 \ge \cdots \ge l_C$ be the eigenvalues of the sample covariance of $n$ observations with population eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_C$, and $\hat F(k) = \sum_{i \le k} l_i / \sum_i l_i$. Then, to first order in $1/n$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}\,\hat F(k) - F(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{n\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma} \sum_{i \le k} \sum_{j > k} \frac{\lambda_i \lambda_j}{\lambda_i - \lambda_j} \;\ge\; 0,
\]
and if $\lambda_{k+1} \le \lambda_k / 2$ then $\mathbb{E}\,\hat F(k) - F(k) \le (2k/n)\,(1 - F(k)) \le (2C/n)\,(1 - F(k))$.
\end{proposition}

The captured fraction is biased upward and $k^*(\tau)$ downward, at
$n/C = 50$ by at most $4\%$ of the residual $1 - \tau$ under a factor-two
gap at $k$. The gap is an assumption; the double sum is not, and on the
measured spectra of ResNet-50 the bias at $n = 50\,C$ and $\tau = 0.999$ is
$1.8\%$ of the residual in the median and $3.3\%$ at most, while
$k^*(0.999)$ at $n/C = 50$ equals its final value on 40 of the 53 layers
and differs by one component on the rest. The gate is therefore the sample
size at which the first-order bias falls below a few per cent of what the
statistic measures. The proof is in the supplement.

With $p = 16$ positions over $50{,}000$ images every convolutional layer
receives $n = 8 \times 10^5$ observations and $n/C$ from $391$ to
$50{,}000$, clearing the gate by a factor of at least seven. Convergence in
$n/C \in \{1, \ldots, 250\}$ is recomputed within the same pass at no extra
cost (supplement, Fig.~S1); the flattening criterion flags 14 of the 703
dense layers, 13 of them single-channel moves at $C \le 32$, and removing
them changes no count below. The pooled estimator is in a different
position: $n$ is the image count, $n/C$ runs from $781$ down to $24.4$, and
eleven of ResNet-50's 53 layers, the $1024$- and $2048$-channel ones, fail
the gate even at $50{,}000$ images. We do not compare estimators there, and
the same limit binds the published pooled measurements: no dataset under
$10^5$ images estimates a $2048$-channel layer at $n/C \ge 50$.

In images rather than in $n/C$, six ResNet-50 checkpoints measured on a
fixed $6{,}400$-image subset ($n/C = 50$ at $C = 2048$) and on the full
$50{,}000$ agree over all 53 layers at Spearman $\rho \ge 0.999$ with a
maximum difference in $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ of $0.016$. The several-seed
comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:learned} are therefore run on the subset;
the pooled statistic does not share this property and is not.

Two measurements were made. The first covers 19 public ImageNet-1k
checkpoints of twelve architectures from \texttt{torchvision} and
\texttt{timm}, every convolutional layer, on the full validation split
\cite{deng2009imagenet} (Table~\ref{tab:models}); with two untrained
controls and one checkpoint loaded through both libraries as a backend check
(it agrees layer for layer and is counted once), 22 runs. Six are ResNet-50
\cite{he2016resnet} under different recipes (the two \texttt{torchvision}
recipes, Wightman's A1 and A3, GluonCV, and semi-supervised YFCC100M
pre-training), so that one architecture is observed under six independent
optimisations and a between-checkpoint spread can be put beside every
within-checkpoint difference. Five exercise the bound at other expansion
ratios: Wide-ResNet-50-2 and ResNeXt-50 (ratio two), MobileNetV2,
MobileNetV3-Large and EfficientNet-B0 (ratio six); ResNet-101 adds 33
bottleneck blocks. The rest are VGG-16 with and without BN, ResNet-18 and
ResNet-34, DenseNet-121 \cite{huang2017densenet} and ConvNeXt-T
\cite{liu2022convnext} under two pre-trainings.

The second measurement asks what training changes and needs several
trained checkpoints and several random initialisations per architecture on
the same images. It was run on the $6{,}400$-image subset over eleven
families and 44 trained checkpoints: ResNet-50 (six recipes), ResNet-18
(six), ResNet-34 (five), ResNet-101 (five), Wide-ResNet-50-2 (three),
ResNeXt-50 (five), VGG-16 (two), DenseNet-121 (two), MobileNetV2 (one),
MobileNetV3-Large (four) and EfficientNet-B0 (five), with three random
seeds each (five for ResNet-50) from each library's default initialiser.
The families span five block types: plain, basic residual, bottleneck at
two depths, two widths and two cardinalities, densely connected, and
inverted residual. ConvNeXt-T, with four dense convolutions, is excluded.

Three tensors are recorded per layer: the convolution output, the
post-activation output and, for residual networks, the block output after
the shortcut and the nonlinearity. Depthwise convolutions do not mix
channels, so their covariance is a different object; they are flagged and
never placed on the same depth axis as dense ones. The untrained control
separates structure the network acquired from structure implied by the
architecture and the second-order statistics of natural images
\cite{field1987relations}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The plan fixed, before any ImageNet data were collected, the primary
statistic ($k^*(0.95)/C$), the gate, and four hypotheses about the
\emph{shape} of the profile, each to be decided by a rank correlation over
the last third of the layers. That rule has almost no power (for VGG-16 it
is four points, exact one-sided $p = 0.167$ for the strongest collapse in
the data) and is not used. Every claim below is a paired comparison over
\emph{all} gated layers, every effect size stands beside the adjacent-layer
step of the curve it is measured on, each subsection is labelled
\emph{pre-registered} or \emph{exploratory}, and every number is produced
by a script from the released spectra.

Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg} validates the protocol against both published profiles. Sections~\ref{sec:rmax}--\ref{sec:blockout} quantify the three choices; Sections~\ref{sec:metrics}--\ref{sec:dwsplit} settle the remaining registered comparisons. Section~\ref{sec:learned} compares trained networks with their initialisations, Section~\ref{sec:decompose} decomposes the statistic into its kernel and data factors, and Sections~\ref{sec:projection}, \ref{sec:diagnostic} and \ref{sec:a18} put the measurement to use.

\subsection{Validation Against Published Profiles}
\label{sec:repro-garg}

Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort} publish $k^*(0.999)/C$ for the
thirteen convolutions of a VGG-16 (BN) trained on CIFAR-10
\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar}. We trained the same architecture on the same
data (100 epochs, one-cycle; $94.1\%$ test accuracy) and measured it at
their threshold. The profiles agree at Pearson $r = 0.981$, Spearman
$0.988$ and a mean absolute difference of $0.052$ against a registered
criterion of $r > 0.9$ and MAD $< 0.10$; the two largest differences,
\texttt{features.27} ($0.73$ against $0.53$) and \texttt{features.30}
($0.52$ against $0.31$), are consistent with a recipe their paper does not
fully specify. Our first attempt, their CIFAR-10 table against our
ImageNet-trained VGG-16, gave $r = 0.125$: the last three layers of an
ImageNet VGG sit above $0.9$ where theirs sit at $0.07$--$0.08$. A CIFAR-10
profile and an ImageNet profile of one architecture are different objects,
which is why every cross-architecture comparison below is made within one
dataset.

Elmoznino and Bonner \cite{elmoznino2024highDim} release the participation
ratio of the pooled block output of the \texttt{torchvision} ResNet-50 on
$10{,}000$ resized validation images. Ours on $50{,}000$ centre-cropped
images matches over all 16 blocks at $\rho = 0.997$ with a median
$|\Delta \log|$ of $0.063$, and $0.985$ at initialisation. The images
differ, so this is agreement between two implementations of one estimator
rather than a reproduction. We conclude that the pipeline is fixed against
both published estimators at both tensors.

\subsection{Normalising by Attainable Rank}
\label{sec:rmax}

\emph{Exploratory; full validation set.} Under the nominal denominator
ResNet-50 has the lowest median $k^*(0.95)/C$ of the first-round networks
($0.25$ against $0.42$ for VGG-16 and $0.51$ for ResNet-18) and a sawtooth
profile: by width, medians $0.23$, $0.34$, $0.40$, $0.37$ for
$C = 64 \ldots 512$, then $0.11$ and $0.13$ at $1024$ and $2048$. Two
readings are possible: the widest layers use a tenth of their width, or the
bound of Equation~\eqref{eq:rmax} explains the reading, since 20 of the 53
convolutions are $1\!\times\!1$ expansions with $r_{\max}/C = 0.25$. The
22 runs test the second reading three ways.

\emph{The bound is never exceeded.} $k^*(0.999)/r_{\max}$ over all layers
of all 22 runs has maximum $0.960$--$1.000$. (Equation~\eqref{eq:rmax} was
got wrong twice during this work; this check caught both.)

\emph{Bounded layers fill the bound.} Table~\ref{tab:bounds} pools the 261
bounded layers of the trained checkpoints by $r_{\max}/C$: the nominal
ratio follows the bound (Spearman $+0.77$) while the attainable ratio sits
at $82$--$99\%$ of the ceiling, within the range of the unbounded layers.

\emph{Within a model the two populations merge.} Bounded against unbounded
layers differ under $C$ in all 14 models with expansion layers
(Mann--Whitney $p < 10^{-2}$, below $10^{-8}$ in eleven) and in none under
$r_{\max}$ ($p > 0.01$ in all 14; smallest $0.025$, ResNet-34's three
downsampling shortcuts). ResNet-50's medians move from $0.236$ against
$0.938$ to $0.927$ against $0.938$, and its median $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ of
$0.42$ is in line with VGG-16 ($0.42$) and ResNet-18 ($0.53$).

We conclude that a trained $1\!\times\!1$ expansion is essentially
full-rank in its input space ($94\%$ of the bound at ratio four, $99\%$ at
six), that the effect Han \emph{et al.} \cite{han2021rexnet} saw in random
networks is, for trained ones, the bound and nothing else, and that
$k^*/C$ at such a layer measures the architecture rather than the
representation. The bound is necessary and not sufficient: the stems of
VGG-16 ($r_{\max} = 27$) and the MobileNets ($27$ of $32$) reach only
$0.3$--$0.4$ of it at $\tau = 0.999$, because natural-image patches are
themselves low-rank. The denominator also decides what a comparison with
initialisation measures: against a random ResNet-50, $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$
finds no agreement in ordering ($\rho = -0.07$) while $\mathrm{PR}/C$,
$\mathrm{erank}/C$ and $k^*(0.95)/C$ find $0.55$--$0.58$, and what the
three share with the random network is the expansion sawtooth.

\subsection{Pooled Versus Position-Sampled Covariance}
\label{sec:estimator}

\emph{Pre-registered; full validation set.} The registered hypothesis was
that the estimator changes the \emph{shape} of the profile and so accounts
for the published disagreement. Both estimators were computed on the same
forward pass and layers.

\emph{Level.} Over the 19 trained checkpoints the pooled $k^*(0.95)/C$ is
strictly lower on $687$ of $703$ dense and $79$ of $80$ depthwise
convolutions passing the pooled gate. Of the 16 dense exceptions 14 are
ties (ten in DenseNet-121 at $C = 32$, where one channel is $0.031$ of the
range, four at the first bottleneck of ResNet-50 recipes) and two are
reversals of one and 23 channels. Table~\ref{tab:pooling} puts the drop
beside each profile's own step under both denominators. Under $C$ the
inverted-residual networks appear to show almost nothing (drop
$0.06$--$0.09$ against a step of $0.5$--$0.7$), but that step is the $1/6$
expansion sawtooth of Section~\ref{sec:rmax}; under $r_{\max}$ the same
networks drop by $1.25$--$2.1$ times their step, in line with the others
(median $2.2$). DenseNet-121 alone stays below one, its step being the
structural alternation of $128$-channel $1\!\times\!1$ and $32$-channel
$3\!\times\!3$ layers.

\emph{Shape.} The registered test, a rank correlation between depth and
the pooled-minus-sampled difference over all gated layers, is not
significant in any of the three first-round architectures with enough
dense layers (VGG-16 $\rho = -0.35$, $p = 0.24$; ResNet-18 $+0.26$,
$p = 0.26$; ResNet-50 $+0.02$, $p = 0.87$), and for VGG-16 the sign is
opposite to the one a ``pooling removes the terminal collapse'' account
needs. The registered hypothesis is not supported. What holds is narrower
and more useful: pooling averages away the within-image spatial variance
and lowers the profile at every layer by more than the profile's own step,
so the two published literatures' numbers are not on one scale whatever
their shapes. Two limits: the pooled gate excludes the $1024$- and
$2048$-channel layers (eleven of ResNet-50's 53), where the difference
would be largest; and adjacent layers are correlated, so the per-checkpoint
Wilcoxon $p$-values (at most $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$) are optimistic and the
counts carry the claim.

\subsection{Convolution Output Versus Block Output}
\label{sec:blockout}

\emph{Exploratory; full validation set.} The bound binds the expansion
convolution's output and not the block output, which adds the shortcut and
rectifies. Both were recorded for all $161$ bottleneck blocks of the nine
bottleneck networks. In every block the block output exceeds the rank bound
of its own last convolution: at $\tau = 0.999$ the expansion output has
median $k^*/C$ of $0.23$ (ceiling $0.25$) in the ResNet-50 and ResNet-101
checkpoints and the block output $0.96$--$0.99$, a factor of $4.1$, the
expansion ratio; in Wide-ResNet-50-2 and ResNeXt-50 (ceiling $0.5$) the
factor is $2.3$--$2.4$; under $k^*(0.95)/C$ the values rise from
$0.09$--$0.19$ to $0.58$--$0.72$, higher in $161$ of $161$. Along the depth
of ResNet-50 the expansion outputs sit at their ceiling from the second
block on while the block outputs rise from $0.72$--$0.92$ to $0.99$
(supplement, Fig.~S2), so the block-level profile of a bottleneck network
is a shallow rise toward full rank, the shape \cite{elmoznino2024highDim}
report at this tensor. Neither tensor is wrong, but the two profiles differ
by the expansion ratio at 20 of ResNet-50's 53 layers, and a profile that
does not state its tensor is not comparable with one read at the other.

\subsection{Agreement Between Summary Statistics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\emph{Pre-registered; full validation set.} The plan asked whether
$k^*(0.95)$ and the participation ratio order the layers of a network the
same way and predicted they need not, PR being set by the head of the
spectrum and $k^*$ counting to its tail. The registered summary, recipe
agreement across the six ResNet-50 checkpoints, is $\rho = 0.85$ for
$k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$, $0.86$ for $\mathrm{PR}/C$ and $0.89$ for
$\mathrm{erank}/C$: each statistic gives a reproducible profile.

\emph{Exploratory (plan Section~11).} Reproducible is not interchangeable.
On the $894$ gate-passing dense convolutions of 21 checkpoints we computed
PR, its finite-sample correction \cite{chun2026estimating}, effective rank,
RankMe \cite{garrido2023rankme} and the $\alpha$-ReQ exponent
\cite{agrawal2022alphaReq} from the same spectra (supplement, Table~S3).
Within a network PR and the ruler do not order layers alike: the
per-network $\rho$ between $\mathrm{PR}/r_{\max}$ and
$k^*(0.999)/r_{\max}$ has median $0.41$, reaches $0.8$ in one network of
eighteen, and is $-0.52$ on the ImageNet VGG-16, where PR falls from
$0.19$ to $0.02$ of $r_{\max}$ while $k^*(0.999)$ rises from $0.44$ to
$0.96$. Effective rank sits between ($0.63$). RankMe orders layers as the
ruler does ($0.94$ against $k^*(0.999)$, $0.99$ against $k^*(0.95)$, above
$0.8$ in seventeen of eighteen). The exponent $\alpha$ is the most stable
quantity across recipes ($0.97$) and correlates with the ruler at $-0.66$,
as a decay rate should, but is a slope and cannot be put on the width
axis. The finite-sample correction changes PR by at most $0.03\%$ at the
gate, which is Proposition~\ref{prop:gate} again, and the pooling count of
Section~\ref{sec:estimator} is the same under every statistic
($687$, $669$ and $688$ of $703$). We conclude that the registered
hypothesis holds in its mechanism and fails in its predicted direction:
head-weighted statistics do reorder layers, but on a plain network the PR
profile falls with depth rather than rising, so the statistic explains
part of the published disagreement and not its sign. Every conclusion
stated below for $k^*(\tau)/r_{\max}$ transfers to RankMe and to the
pooling count, and not to PR or $\alpha$ without re-measurement.

\subsection{Threshold Sensitivity}
\label{sec:threshold}

\emph{Pre-registered; full validation set.} Over
$\tau \in \{0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999\}$ on VGG-16 the level moves a great
deal (the last layer reads $0.29$, $0.44$, $0.74$, $0.95$) and the layers
rise, dip and recover at the same places (supplement, Fig.~S4). Across the
six ResNet-50 recipes the between-recipe spread narrows with $\tau$ in the
expected direction: at $0.999$ the per-layer SD is $0.019$ against a step
of $0.033$ and the profile mean is $0.86$--$0.92$ of attainable rank, so
nearly every layer is close to full rank and little is left to order. The
threshold sets how much of the tail is counted; it does not reorder.

\subsection{Depthwise and Dense Convolutions}
\label{sec:dwsplit}

\emph{Exploratory; full set and subset.} A depthwise layer does not mix
channels, and three architectures show why the two kinds cannot share an
axis. Eighteen of ConvNeXt-T's 22 convolutions are depthwise, four dense,
and the groups do not overlap (median $k^*(0.95)/C$ $0.71$ against
$0.31$); in MobileNetV3-Large and EfficientNet-B0 the sign reverses, the
depthwise layers at $0.19$ and $0.16$ of $r_{\max}$ against $0.51$ and
$0.56$ for the $1\!\times\!1$ layers. Interleaved, either pair produces a
sawtooth that is not a property of the representation. Kept apart, the
split also weakens ConvNeXt-T's trained-versus-untrained comparison: its
four dense convolutions are all higher than at initialisation in both
checkpoints ($p = 0.06$ with four layers), its depthwise ones higher in
$13$ ($p = 0.013$) and $12$ ($p = 0.21$) of 18. A first-round test that
pooled the two kinds gave $p = 6 \times 10^{-4}$; it is withdrawn.

\subsection{Trained Versus Initialised Profiles}
\label{sec:learned}

\emph{Exploratory; full validation set for the paired test, the $6{,}400$-image subset with several seeds for everything else.}\label{sec:trained} The profiles are not implied by architecture and input statistics alone, and for ResNet-50 the statement has an error bar. Against
one random initialisation on the full validation set, each of the six
trained recipes is higher on $45$ to $50$ of its 53 dense convolutions
(Wilcoxon $p \le 4 \times 10^{-8}$ in every case). The median difference in
$k^*(0.95)/C$ is $+0.10$ to $+0.20$, which is \emph{smaller} than those
profiles' adjacent-layer step of $0.20$--$0.27$; in units of attainable rank, on the $6{,}400$-image subset with five random seeds (Section~\ref{sec:models}), the median level is $0.23$ at initialisation and $0.47$ trained, against a step of $0.09$. The paired
test over all layers is what carries the claim; the effect size under $C$
would not carry it alone.


That a trained profile is higher than an untrained one says nothing about whether it has the same \emph{shape}. The second
measurement of Section~\ref{sec:models} asks that: for each family, several
trained checkpoints and several random initialisations were measured on the
same $6{,}400$ images, and three rank correlations between $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$
profiles were computed over the family's dense convolutions. Random against
random (R--R) is the ceiling a comparison with a single random draw could
reach; trained against trained (T--T) says whether the recipes share a
profile at all; trained against random (T--R) is the question. A family in
which T--R is close to R--R has a profile whose ordering is set by the
architecture; one in which T--R is far below both has learned its ordering.
Table~\ref{tab:families} gives the medians, with the median level of the
trained and random profiles.

Three behaviours appear in the families measured, and they sort by block type; whether the block is the cause is taken up below, with a controlled experiment that says it is not.

\emph{Plain, basic-residual and densely connected networks learn a level, not an ordering.} This is the case Ansuini \emph{et al.} \cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} saw in VGG-16, where the linear PC-ID profile was qualitatively unchanged by training. For VGG-16, ResNet-18, ResNet-34 and DenseNet-121 the
trained profiles agree with the random ones at $\rho = 0.61$--$0.75$, against
ceilings of $0.79$--$0.98$, and training raises the median level by $0.09$ (VGG) to $0.19$ (ResNet-18). The profiles' own adjacent-layer steps are $0.05$, $0.14$, $0.10$ and $0.30$ (Table~\ref{tab:pooling}), so the rise exceeds the step in all but DenseNet-121, whose step is the structural $1\!\times\!1$/$3\!\times\!3$ alternation noted in Section~\ref{sec:estimator}.

\emph{The ImageNet ResNet-50 checkpoints learn their ordering.} The six recipes agree with
each other ($0.84$) and with initialisation not at all ($-0.08$, range
$-0.31$ to $+0.33$ over 30 pairs), against a random-vs-random ceiling of
$0.94$; the five random draws are highly reproducible, so the disagreement
is not draw noise. This does not contradict the observation of Daneshmand \emph{et al.} \cite{daneshmand2020bn} that gradient descent largely preserves the initial rank of the \emph{last} hidden layer: their object is one layer's rank across networks of different depth, ours is the ordering of layers within one network, and the level of ResNet-50 does rise rather than fall. The trend with depth reverses: every random draw falls with depth (writing $\rho_{\rm depth}$ for the Spearman correlation of a profile with layer index: $-0.53$ to $-0.81$) and every trained
recipe rises (mean $+0.56$). Part of that disagreement is the trend itself: a sign flip in one monotone trend gives $\rho \approx 0$ over 53 layers without any reordering inside a stage. Ranking layers within each stage, where all share $C$ and no width or bound confound is possible, and pooling the ranks (column T--R$_{\rm stage}$ of Table~\ref{tab:families}) removes the trend: ResNet-50's within-stage agreement with initialisation is $0.27$ against a ceiling of $0.75$, ResNet-18's is $0.75$ against $0.92$ and ResNet-34's $0.55$ against $0.87$. The bottleneck network reorders its layers within a stage as well, but less completely than the whole-profile figure suggests.

\emph{Inverted-residual checkpoints agree with one another weakly or not at all.} The four MobileNetV3-Large checkpoints do not share a profile (T--T $-0.18$) although their random draws do ($0.75$); the five EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints, all trained on ImageNet-1k alone, agree only moderately ($0.40$, against a random ceiling of $0.97$); MobileNetV2 has a single public checkpoint. What varies most is the level: the median $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ of the $1\!\times\!1$ layers is $0.18$, $0.45$, $0.59$ and $0.65$ across the MobileNetV3 checkpoints and $0.10$, $0.26$, $0.70$, $0.71$ and $0.73$ across the EfficientNet-B0 ones, a $3.6$-fold and a $7$-fold range under one architecture and one dataset. The depthwise layers move against the rest of the study in two of the three families: in the median over checkpoints training roughly halves their effective width in MobileNetV3 ($0.41 \to 0.20$) and EfficientNet-B0 ($0.35 \to 0.22$), with trained values spread from $0.05$ to $0.34$, while MobileNetV2's single checkpoint leaves them where initialisation put them ($0.39 \to 0.42$).

Two further checks bear on the reading. Under the nominal denominator the same comparison gives T--R of $0.57$ for ResNet-50 against $0.67$ (ResNet-34) and $0.81$ (ResNet-18): the contrast survives but shrinks, because what the trained and random bottleneck profiles share under $C$ is the expansion sawtooth, which is the architecture (Section~\ref{sec:rmax}). And the families with an architectural ordering are also the shallower ones, so depth could stand in for block type. Three further bottleneck families separate the two. ResNet-101, twice as deep as ResNet-50, agrees with its initialisation \emph{more}, not less (T--R $0.20$, within-stage $0.32$); Wide-ResNet-50-2 and ResNeXt-50, the same depth as ResNet-50 with expansion ratio two, agree more still ($0.33$ and $0.46$; within-stage $0.57$ and $0.66$). All four bottleneck families lie below every plain, basic and dense family ($0.61$--$0.75$), and the four order as a gradient rather than a class: the ordering is least architectural in the deepest-and-narrowest bottleneck and more so as the block is widened or given cardinality. Depth alone does not produce the effect, since the deeper network shows less of it.

A natural hypothesis, that training reorders the $1\!\times\!1$
channel-mixing layers and leaves the $3\!\times\!3$ layers where the
architecture put them, does not survive the role split. In ResNet-50 both
kinds are reordered (T--R $+0.09$ for $3\!\times\!3$, $-0.28$ for
$1\!\times\!1$); in DenseNet-121 the $3\!\times\!3$ layers are the ones
that move ($-0.26$) while the $1\!\times\!1$ layers stay ($+0.39$). What
sorts the ImageNet families is the block, not the kernel.

\emph{Is the block the cause? Under one recipe on CIFAR-10 it is not.} The
released plan (its Section~9.1) registered, before any of its data were
seen, the experiment that separates the block from everything that varies
between public checkpoints: with the recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg}, a basic-block ResNet $[6,6,6,6]$ and a
bottleneck ResNet-50 $[3,4,6,3]$ with the same stage widths
($64$--$512$ at the block input), the same CIFAR stem and the same number of
convolutions ($52$ and $53$), and a CIFAR variant of MobileNetV2, three
seeds each, measured at initialisation, at 17 points along training and at
the end, on the CIFAR-10 test images. The registered hypothesis H9.1 was
that the bottleneck network's trained-versus-random agreement lies below the
basic network's (every pair, or Mann--Whitney $p < 0.01$); its registered
consequence, if it failed, was that the ImageNet contrast is attributed to
depth or recipe and this section is rewritten accordingly.
Table~\ref{tab:round3} gives the outcome. H9.1 fails: the basic network
agrees with its initialisation at $\rho = 0.55$ ($0.46$--$0.57$ over the
nine trained-random pairs), the bottleneck network at $0.50$
($0.38$--$0.54$), $p = 0.2$, and the MobileNetV2 at $0.44$; under one recipe
at this scale the three block types keep the same share of their initial
ordering. The other two ImageNet signatures of ResNet-50 are absent as well:
the level of the CIFAR bottleneck network \emph{falls} on training ($0.20
\to 0.16$; the basic network rises, $0.21 \to 0.32$, as its ImageNet
relatives do), and its depth trend flattens ($-0.78 \to -0.21$) rather than
reversing. The registered attribution therefore stands: what makes the six
ImageNet ResNet-50 recipes reorder their layers is not the bottleneck block
as such but something they share and this experiment lacks, the dataset or the
ImageNet recipes, which the experiment does not separate (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); the four-family
gradient above already excludes depth alone. Two exploratory readings
survive. Within stages the bottleneck network does reorder more than the
basic one (T--R$_{\rm stage}$ $0.25$ against $0.50$), the same direction as
on ImageNet ($0.27$ against $0.75$), but this comparison was not registered
and rests on three seeds. And the second registered hypothesis, H9.2, holds:
the three MobileNetV2 seeds share their trained profile at $\rho =
0.99$--$1.00$ against a random-versus-random ceiling of $0.68$, so an
inverted-residual network trained under one recipe does have a profile, and
the disagreement among the public MobileNetV3 and EfficientNet checkpoints
is the recipes' doing.

Three cautions. The threshold sets how much room this comparison has: at $\tau = 0.999$ the
trained-versus-random agreement for ResNet-50 is $0.25$ rather than
$-0.07$, because the ceiling compresses both. The inverted-residual checkpoints differ in more than the optimiser: one MobileNetV3 was pre-trained on ImageNet-21k and the longest-trained checkpoints ran for $3{,}600$ epochs, and even the five same-dataset EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints span three training frameworks. ``Weak agreement'' is therefore a statement about the checkpoints one can download; the same-recipe, several-seed study registered in the released plan (the CIFAR-10 MobileNetV2 of Table~\ref{tab:round3}) finds the seeds agreeing at $0.99$--$1.00$, so it is not a property of the block. That is also the practical content of the finding: a single MobileNet or EfficientNet checkpoint does not stand for its architecture.
And MobileNetV3's random draws agree only at $0.75$ ($0.37$ for the
depthwise layers), so its random profile is itself unstable, possibly a
consequence of its hard-swish nonlinearity and squeeze-excitation blocks.

\emph{When does the profile become the trained one?} One training run of
VGG-16 with BN on CIFAR-10 was measured at 17 points along its 100 epochs
(one seed; a second run of the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg}, reaching $94.0\%$; Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}).
The profile leaves its
initialisation in the first epochs ($\rho$ against the initial profile
falls to $0.72$ by epoch 5 while the level overshoots to $0.47$), returns
towards it during the high-learning-rate phase, and is in place by epoch
40: $\rho$ against the final profile is $0.98$ from epoch 40 onwards, while
test accuracy still rises from $84\%$ to $94\%$. The final profile agrees
with the initial one at $\rho = 0.93$, consistent with the plain-network
row of Table~\ref{tab:families} and stronger. For a plain network, then, the ordering of layers is settled before the fit is, and it is largely the ordering the architecture began with (the controlled runs below extend this to the other two block types); the level moves earlier and non-monotonically, as the effective rank of penultimate features does in Huh \emph{et al.} \cite{huh2023lowRank} and the manifold dimension of deep layers does late in training in Stephenson \emph{et al.} \cite{stephenson2021geometry}, and Masarczyk \emph{et al.} \cite{masarczyk2023tunnel} report the rank collapse of the deeper layers within the first few hundred steps. What settles at epoch 40 in our run is the Spearman ordering under a one-cycle schedule whose peak learning rate falls at epoch 30; a different schedule could settle at a different epoch.

The nine runs of the controlled experiment, under the same schedule, give
the same picture with seeds (Table~\ref{tab:round3}, medians over three
seeds). All three architectures leave their initialisation in the first
epoch, and further than VGG-16 does: after one epoch the profile is
\emph{anti}-correlated with the initial one ($\rho = -0.50$ for both
ResNets), the depth trend has reversed from falling ($-0.90$, $-0.78$) to
rising ($+0.69$, $+0.86$) and the level has doubled or tripled ($0.22 \to
0.63$, $0.20 \to 0.37$). The rising trend of the trained ImageNet ResNet-50
is thus present transiently in every CIFAR-10 network, basic or bottleneck,
and is undone during the high-learning-rate phase: by epoch 15--20 the
trend is falling again and the agreement with initialisation has climbed
back to $0.6$--$0.9$, from where it drifts down to its final $0.50$--$0.54$.
The profile then settles at epoch 60 (basic), 40 (bottleneck) and 40
(MobileNetV2) while accuracy is still $84$--$88\%$ on its way to
$93$--$95\%$. The bottleneck trajectory that the VGG-16 run could not supply
therefore shows no late reordering: on CIFAR-10 the bottleneck network's
ordering is in place as early as the others' and ends as close to
initialisation as theirs.

Along this CIFAR-10 run the profile also falls with depth from epoch 10 onwards ($\rho_{\rm depth}$ $-0.6$ to $-0.9$; only $-0.2$ during the first epochs), and the last three layers end at $k^*(0.95)/C$ of $0.05$--$0.07$ ($0.08$ at $\tau = 0.999$): the terminal collapse of \cite{garg2019lowEffort} is present in our own CIFAR-10 network and absent in our ImageNet VGG-16, where the same layers read $0.44$--$0.53$ ($0.92$--$0.97$ at $\tau = 0.999$). Neural
collapse \cite{papyan2020neuralCollapse} predicts a penultimate rank of
$C_{\rm class} - 1$, nine on CIFAR-10, but the CIFAR head is also
shallower than the ImageNet one. The class-count control of
Section~\ref{sec:a18} varies the class count at fixed head depth and finds
the collapse closing monotonically as classes are added and deepening with
optimisation time; head depth at fixed class count was not varied
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Functional Calibration by Truncation}
\label{sec:projection}

\emph{Exploratory; the $6{,}400$-image subset.} The statistic counts
directions of variance. Whether the directions it discards carry function
can be checked at inference by an operation that is itself well known:
replacing each convolution output $y$ by $\bar y + U_k U_k^{\top}(y - \bar y)$,
with $U_k$ the top-$k$ principal channel directions of that output, is the
response-PCA truncation of Zhang \emph{et al.}
\cite{zhang2015efficient,zhang2016accelerating} (their Eq.~(4), the linear
form of their method), which they fold into the kernel as a low-rank
factorisation and apply, with a per-layer rank chosen under a speed budget
and usually followed by fine-tuning, to SPP-net and VGG-16. Energy
thresholds as a rank rule appear there and as baselines in later work
\cite{liebenwein2021alds}, and Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort}
retrain at their $99.9\%$ criterion. The closest precedent for reading a dimension count off accuracy is the ``classification dimension'' of Feng \emph{et al.} \cite{feng2022rankDiminishing}, the number of principal components of a layer that preserves $95\%$ of accuracy, measured one layer at a time. We use the truncation for one purpose only: with $k = k^*(\tau)$ from the measurement, at every dense convolution at once, with no fine-tuning and no rebuilding, does accuracy survive?

What the answer should depend on is fixed to first order by the discarded variance.

\begin{proposition}[first-order effect of truncation]
\label{prop:truncation}
Let $z$ be the output of layer $\ell$ at a position, $\Sigma_\ell = \sum_i \lambda_i u_i u_i^{\top}$ its covariance, $r = (I - U_k U_k^{\top})(z - \bar z)$ the discarded component, and $J_\ell$ the Jacobian of the logits with respect to $z$. Then $\mathbb{E}\|r\|^2 = (1 - \tau_k)\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_\ell$ with $\tau_k$ the captured fraction, and to first order
\[
  \mathbb{E}\|J_\ell r\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_\ell \Sigma_\ell^{\perp}(k) J_\ell^{\top}\bigr) \le \|J_\ell\|_{\rm op}^2 (1 - \tau_k) \operatorname{tr}\Sigma_\ell,
\]
where $\Sigma_\ell^{\perp}(k) = \sum_{i > k} \lambda_i u_i u_i^{\top}$; truncating $L$ layers at once perturbs the logits by at most $\sum_\ell (\mathbb{E}\|J_\ell r_\ell\|^2)^{1/2}$ in root mean square.
\end{proposition}

The proposition does not predict an accuracy, since that depends on the logit margins and on the nonlinearity beyond first order, but it fixes the scale: between $\tau = 0.999$ and $0.95$ the discarded energy per layer differs by a factor of $50$ and its amplitude by $7$, and the layers add. A criterion that keeps $0.1\%$ of the variance at each of 53 layers and one that keeps $95\%$ are therefore not two settings of one knob but an order of magnitude apart in what they inject, which is what the measurement below finds. The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The first-order term was evaluated directly on the A1 recipe (64 held-out images, covariances from 256 others): for each of the 53 convolutions, the logit change under truncation of that layer alone was compared with its linearisation along the discarded component. Across layers the two agree in rank order at $\rho = 0.99$--$1.00$ at every $\tau$, and the linear term overestimates the actual change by a factor of $1.2$--$1.3$ in the median, so the first-order picture holds even at $\tau = 0.95$ where the perturbation is large. The layers that dominate are the same three the diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} finds under-used, the stem, the first expansion of stage~1 and its projection shortcut, and the second of them is the layer the leave-one-layer-out test below identifies. At $\tau = 0.999$ the largest single-layer effect ($98$) is of the order of the median squared logit margin ($55$), which is where an accuracy loss of the size this recipe shows becomes possible; at $0.95$ it exceeds the margin by two orders of magnitude.
On one half of the subset the channel covariance of every convolution output of eight checkpoints (the six ResNet-50 recipes, ResNet-18, VGG-16 with BN) was accumulated exactly as in the measurement; on the other half the network was evaluated with every convolution output truncated. The control truncates each layer to $k^*(\tau)$
random orthonormal directions instead. Table~\ref{tab:projection} gives top-1 accuracy, and since truncated and untruncated networks are scored on the same images, each difference has an exact McNemar test; ``unchanged'' below means that test does not reject at $p = 0.01$.

At $\tau = 0.999$ the truncation keeps $84$--$93\%$ of each layer's
attainable rank ($63$--$86\%$ of nominal width), and in six of the eight
checkpoints accuracy is unchanged: ResNet-50 under the \texttt{torchvision}
V1 and V2, GluonCV and semi-supervised recipes, ResNet-18 and VGG-16 move
by $-0.3$ to $+0.2$ points (McNemar $p \ge 0.15$). The two exceptions are
the ResNet-50 checkpoints trained with the A1 and A3 recipes of Wightman
\emph{et al.} \cite{wightman2021rsb}, which lose $1.7$ points each
($p < 10^{-4}$). The same architecture, the same data and the same $k^*$
therefore give different answers depending on the recipe: the RSB recipes
(binary cross-entropy, heavy augmentation) place function in channel
directions that hold less than $0.1\%$ of the variance, and the
conventional recipes do not. In every checkpoint the directions beyond
$k^*(0.999)$ carry at most a few points of accuracy, and random directions
of the same dimension give chance throughout, so what is retained is the
principal subspace and not merely the dimension count.

Below $\tau = 0.999$ the cost depends on the recipe far more than on the
architecture. At $0.99$ four checkpoints lose $1.6$--$5.2$ points while A1
and A3 fall to $11\%$ and $34\%$; at $0.95$, the pre-registered threshold,
ResNet-50 V1, ResNet-18 and VGG-16 lose $19$--$21$ points but the other
five ResNet-50 recipes fall to $0.3$--$6.6\%$. Part of that collapse is a
single layer: in the A1 checkpoint \texttt{layer1.0.conv3} holds more than
$99\%$ of its variance in one direction, so it is truncated to one
dimension at $\tau \le 0.99$, and leaving that layer untruncated raises
the A1 network from $11\%$ to $54\%$ at $0.99$; it does not change the
$1.7$-point loss at $0.999$, which is spread over layers. The functional
reading of $k^*$ is therefore threshold-specific and recipe-specific:
$k^*(0.999)$ bounds the width used by conventionally trained networks and
undercounts it by about two points of accuracy for the RSB recipes, and
$k^*(0.95)$, which is what the profiles above are drawn with, orders layers
and tracks training but says nothing about how much width could be
removed.

Two further things this experiment does not show. Read as a compression,
the truncation is the rank-$k$ factorisation $W \mapsto U_k (U_k^{\top} W)$,
which costs $k(d + C_{\rm out})$ multiply-adds per position against
$C_{\rm out} d$; summed over ResNet-50's dense convolutions at
$\tau = 0.999$ it removes only $5\%$ of the convolution FLOPs, because the
$1\!\times\!1$ expansion layers have $d < C_{\rm out}$ and gain nothing from
factorisation, and Zhang \emph{et al.}'s nonlinear and fine-tuned variants
do better at any given speed-up. And it is not a pruning result: a rank
restriction on a trained network is not a narrower network trained from
scratch, and whether the latter would reach the same accuracy is a
different question, which we do not address.

\subsection{Robustness of the Protocol}
\label{sec:robust}

\emph{Exploratory; the $6{,}400$-image subset and $3{,}200$ COCO images.}
Each choice the protocol makes was varied on ResNet-50 (V1) and the
$k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ profile compared with the reference run by Spearman
$\rho$ across layers and by the per-layer difference
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Four choices do not matter at the resolution
of one channel: sampling 4 or 64 positions per image instead of 16,
resizing the image directly to $224 \times 224$ instead of cropping it, and
reading the batch-normalisation output instead of the convolution output
all give $\rho \ge 0.99$ and a median per-layer difference below $0.01$,
the largest single difference being one channel of a 64-channel layer.
The batch-norm result deserves a word, since a per-channel affine map can
in principle reshape the spectrum: in the trained network it does not,
and in the untrained one the two spectra coincide. The protocol's numbers
are therefore not artefacts of the sampling, the preprocessing or the
exact tensor within a conv--BN pair.

Two variations do matter, and they say what the profile is a property of.
Replacing the images by noise changes the trained profile substantially:
white noise gives $\rho = 0.32$ and a median level of $0.24$ against
$0.42$, and $1/f$ noise, which shares the second-order statistics of
natural images, recovers part of it ($\rho = 0.70$, level $0.29$). The
same substitution moves the untrained network's profile much less
($\rho = 0.78$--$0.88$ against its ImageNet profile). A trained profile is
thus a property of the network on natural images, not of the network
alone, which is the data factor of Section~\ref{sec:decompose} seen from
the other side. Changing the natural images themselves, on the other
hand, changes nothing: the same ImageNet-trained ResNet-50 measured on
$3{,}200$ COCO images gives a profile identical to its ImageNet profile
to within one channel at every layer. Finally, the ResNet-50 bodies of
the COCO-trained Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN detectors, measured on the
same COCO images, order their layers like the classification network
($\rho = 0.91$) but at a lower level ($0.29$ and $0.31$ against $0.41$):
the detection task, or its training schedule, uses less of the attainable
width at this criterion. The protocol reads no labels, so it applies to
any convolutional backbone unchanged.

\subsection{Decomposition of the Effective Width}
\label{sec:decompose}

\emph{Exploratory; the $6{,}400$-image subset.} At the tensor this paper
reads, the output of a convolution before its nonlinearity, the channel
covariance is an exact function of two things: the kernel, and the
covariance of the receptive-field patch. Writing $W \in
\mathbb{R}^{C_{\rm out} \times d}$ for the kernel as a matrix, $d = C_{\rm in}
k_h k_w$, and $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ for the $d \times d$ covariance of the
patch vector (the im2col column) at a sampled position,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{\rm out} = W\, \Sigma_{\rm patch}\, W^{\top}.
  \label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The bound of Section~\ref{sec:rmax} is the rank of the right-hand side. Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp} also separates the two factors exactly, and the separation is what the counterfactual below rests on. Write $W = P W_{\circ}$ for the polar decomposition, with $P = (WW^{\top})^{1/2}$ positive semidefinite and $W_{\circ}$ the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix to $W$ (for $W = U S V^{\top}$, $W_{\circ} = U V^{\top}$), and let $r = \operatorname{rank} W$ and $\sigma_{\max} \ge \sigma_{\min} > 0$ be the largest and the smallest nonzero singular value of $W$.

\begin{proposition}[the kernel reweights the counterfactual spectrum]
\label{prop:ostrowski}
Let $\Sigma_{\circ} = W_{\circ} \Sigma_{\rm patch} W_{\circ}^{\top}$. Then
$\Sigma_{\rm out} = P \Sigma_{\circ} P$, and for $i \le r$ there are
$\theta_i \in [\sigma_{\min}^2, \sigma_{\max}^2]$ with
$\lambda_i(\Sigma_{\rm out}) = \theta_i\, \lambda_i(\Sigma_{\circ})$; both
spectra vanish beyond $r$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:kstar}
With $\kappa = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$, $\tau' = \kappa^2\tau / (1 - \tau + \kappa^2 \tau)$ and $\tau'' = \tau / (\kappa^2 (1 - \tau) + \tau)$,
$k^*(\tau''; \Sigma_{\circ}) \le k^*(\tau; \Sigma_{\rm out}) \le k^*(\tau'; \Sigma_{\circ})$ for every $\tau \in (0, 1)$.
If the nonzero singular values of $W$ are equal ($\kappa = 1$) then
$k^*(\tau; \Sigma_{\rm out}) = k^*(\tau; \Sigma_{\circ})$, and if moreover
$C_{\rm out} \ge d$ then $k^*(\tau; \Sigma_{\rm out}) = \min(k^*(\tau; \Sigma_{\rm patch}), r_{\max})$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[the counterfactual is a compression of the data]
\label{prop:compression}
$\Sigma_{\circ}$ is unitarily similar to the compression of $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$
to the row space of $W$, so $\lambda_{i + d - r}(\Sigma_{\rm patch}) \le
\lambda_i(\Sigma_{\circ}) \le \lambda_i(\Sigma_{\rm patch})$ for $i \le r$, with
equality of the nonzero spectra when $r = d$. In particular $\Sigma_{\circ}$
depends on the kernel only through its row space, not through its singular
values.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[the data factor along depth]
\label{prop:depth}
Let $\Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$ be the channel covariance of the
post-activation output of layer $\ell$ at a position, and suppose the
position-sampled patch of layer $\ell + 1$ stacks $k_h k_w$ such vectors,
each almost surely in the range of $\Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$. Then
$\operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm patch}^{(\ell+1)} \le k_h k_w\,
\operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$, and hence
$\operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm out}^{(\ell+1)} \le \min\bigl(C_{\ell+1},\,
k_h k_w \operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}

Proofs are in the appendix; they are linear algebra (the Ostrowski and
Cauchy interlacing theorems). Proposition~\ref{prop:ostrowski} says that the
measured spectrum is the counterfactual spectrum reweighted eigenvalue by
eigenvalue by factors confined to the kernel's singular-value range, so the
kernel acts on the width only through the spread of its singular values,
and Corollary~\ref{cor:kstar} is the exact form of the statement that an
isometric kernel passes the effective width of its input through unchanged
(the orthogonality-regularisation literature's aim, Section~\ref{sec:where},
which reaches the ceiling only if the input is also white).
Proposition~\ref{prop:depth} is the second bound on a layer's width, set by
the data and the preceding layer rather than by the architecture; it is the
bound that binds at initialisation. The propositions give the four quantities below their meaning, and they are checked on the data. Over the 465 dense convolutions of the ten checkpoints in Table~\ref{tab:decompose}, with the numerical rank of $W$ taken at $10^{-6}$ of its largest singular value, the eigenvalue ratios of Proposition~\ref{prop:ostrowski} lie within $[\sigma_{\min}^2, \sigma_{\max}^2]$ on 464 (the exception is a DenseNet-121 layer whose smallest retained singular value sits at the tolerance), and the inequality of Corollary~\ref{cor:kstar} holds on 465 at both $\tau = 0.95$ and $0.999$. The corollary is informative, that is, gives a bound below $r_{\max}$, on 246 of the 465 layers at $\tau = 0.95$; on the rest the condition number $\kappa$ is large enough that $\tau'$ is close to one. The tolerance is not doing the work: repeating the ResNet-50 check at $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-8}$ gives 53 and 51 of 53 layers within the Ostrowski bounds (the two exceptions at $10^{-8}$ are the stem and the first convolution of stage 1, two of the six layers whose numerically null directions that tolerance retains), the corollary holds on every layer at every $\tau$ at all three settings, and the median $\kappa$ is $10.6$ throughout; only its maximum moves, from $166$ to $10^{8}$. Twenty-four layers of the trained checkpoints have numerically null kernel directions (in ResNet-50 all six are in the stem and the first stage), and none of the random initialisation does: training empties some kernel directions outright, which the polar factor $W_{\circ}$ simply omits.

We accumulated $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ for every dense convolution of ten
checkpoints on the subset ($n/d \ge 67$ throughout) and compared four
quantities per layer: the measured $k^*/r_{\max}$, recomputed from
Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp}; the same statistic for $W W^{\top}$, which is
what the layer would show if its patches were white; the statistic for
$\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ itself, capped at $r_{\max}$; and the statistic for
$W_{\circ}\Sigma_{\rm patch}W_{\circ}^{\top}$, where $W_{\circ}$ is the polar
factor of $W$, the nearest (semi-)isometry to the trained kernel. The last
is an analytic counterfactual: the effective width this layer would have if
its kernel were orthogonalised and its input left as it is.
Table~\ref{tab:decompose} gives the medians and the rank correlations across
layers; Fig.~\ref{fig:decompose} shows ResNet-50 layer by layer.

Three things follow. First, the identity is a check on the pipeline:
recomputing $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ from $W$ and an independently accumulated
$\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ reproduces the directly measured value to within
$0.008$ on every layer of the five checkpoints where both exist.

Second, training moves the bottleneck from the data to the kernel. At
initialisation the patch covariance is far from full-rank (median
$0.28$ of $r_{\max}$ for ResNet-50), the random kernel is nearly isotropic
($0.86$), and the measured width follows the data ($\rho = 0.94$) and not
the kernel ($0.44$); orthogonalising the random kernel changes nothing ($0.22$ against $0.20$). The patch covariance of the untrained network also loses rank with depth (Fig.~\ref{fig:decompose}, bottom), which is why every random profile in Section~\ref{sec:learned} falls with depth: at initialisation the profile is a property of how the input statistics degrade through random layers, not of the kernels. In every trained checkpoint the patch covariance
has become full-rank at the $95\%$ criterion (median $1.00$; $0.88$--$0.89$
for the two inverted-residual networks), and the measured width follows the kernel spectrum ($\rho = 0.81$--$0.97$ for ResNet-18, the three ResNet-50 recipes, VGG-16 and DenseNet-121; $0.57$ for ResNet-34; $0.46$ and $0.70$ for EfficientNet-B0 and MobileNetV3) and more weakly the data ($0.24$--$0.63$ for the first six; $0.67$ and $0.78$ for the two inverted-residual networks, whose patch covariance is the one that is not saturated). The ordering of layers in a trained
profile is, to a first approximation, the ordering of the kernels' Gram
spectra.

Third, the level is set jointly. A trained kernel alone, under white
patches, would give $0.69$--$0.86$; the measured value is $0.42$--$0.70$;
orthogonalising the kernel with the measured patches gives
$0.75$--$0.87$, higher than the measured value in $401$ of the $412$ dense convolutions of the nine trained checkpoints at $\tau = 0.95$. The
trained kernel is therefore not merely anisotropic; its high-gain
directions are aligned with the high-variance directions of its input, so
that the two factors compound and the output spectrum is steeper than
either would make it alone. The alignment can be measured directly: with
$W = \sum_i s_i u_i v_i^{\top}$, let $\rho_{\rm align}$ be the Spearman
correlation, over the retained right singular directions, between the
kernel's gain $s_i^2$ and the input variance the direction carries,
$v_i^{\top} \Sigma_{\rm patch} v_i$; it is zero for an isotropic kernel or
white input, positive when the kernel amplifies the input's principal
directions, negative when it suppresses them.

\emph{The alignment, measured (plan Section~9.6; the registered sign was wrong).} The plan registered, before the data, that trained kernels would show $\rho_{\rm align} < 0$, reasoning from the counterfactual above; the reasoning had the sign backwards, since it is amplification of the principal directions, not suppression, that steepens the output spectrum below the isometric counterfactual. As registered the prediction fails in every checkpoint ($0$ of $48$). What the data show, with the same test in the other direction, is reported here as a post-hoc finding. Over the $19$ distinct ImageNet checkpoints of Table~\ref{tab:models}, on the $6{,}400$-image subset at $48$ positions, the median $\rho_{\rm align}$ over a network's dense convolutions is $+0.91$ in the median and above $+0.8$ in $16$ of $19$; the six ResNet-50 recipes give $+0.86$ to $+0.95$, and the sign test is significant at $p < 0.01$ in $43$ of the $48$ trained networks measured (the $19$ plus $29$ CIFAR-10 networks: the $24$ of Section~\ref{sec:learned}, the final networks of Section~\ref{sec:a18}'s arms and the trajectories). At initialisation it is zero: every one of the $20$ random draws has $|\rho_{\rm align}| < 0.1$ in the median. The exceptions are the inverted-residual families, MobileNetV2 $+0.11$ (not significant), MobileNetV3-Large $+0.28$ and EfficientNet-B0 $+0.61$, which are also the families of Section~\ref{sec:learned} whose trained checkpoints share no profile. Along training the alignment rises rather than falls: on the nine CIFAR-10 runs of Section~\ref{sec:a18} measured at seventeen epochs, the layer-median $\rho_{\rm align}$ correlates with epoch at $+0.65$ to $+0.91$, reaches $90\%$ of its final value by epoch $7$--$15$ in every run, before the profile itself settles (epochs $15$--$100$), and is within $10\%$ of its final value at the settle epoch in seven of nine. The representation settles later still: the layer-median CKA of each checkpoint to the final network stays at $0.76$--$0.79$ from epoch $10$ to $50$ and reaches $0.9$ only in the annealing phase (epochs $80$--$90$), so the registered coincidence of the two arrival epochs (P9.23, plan Section~9.9.3) fails in all nine runs; the kernel is aligned to its input long before the representation stops moving. The orthogonality penalties of Section~\ref{sec:learned} do not undo it: paired over the $195$ layers of the SO and untreated arms, $|\rho_{\rm align}|$ differs by $+0.002$ in the median ($p = 0.5$, P9.12 not supported), which is consistent with those penalties leaving the kernels far from isometric. Across families the trained value is not a constant ($+0.11$ to $+0.98$; P9.13), so it is a property that training produces to different degrees, not a fixed point. Fig.~\ref{fig:decompose} and Table~\ref{tab:decompose} are unchanged by this; what changes is the reading: the trained kernel is a matched filter for its input's principal directions, and the measured width sits below the isometric counterfactual because the kernel sharpens the input spectrum rather than flattening it.

This is the quantity the orthogonality-regularisation literature acts on
from the weight side (Section~\ref{sec:where}): Huang \emph{et al.} \cite{huang2018own} show that an orthogonal kernel decorrelates its output when the input is white, and Table~\ref{tab:decompose} shows
that in trained networks the input is not white but is full-rank, so
orthogonalisation would raise the effective width by about $0.3$ of
$r_{\max}$ rather than to the ceiling. That number is a prediction, made
here with the kernels held at their trained values and the patches held
fixed; a network trained with an orthogonality penalty changes both.

\emph{The prediction, tested.} The released plan (its Section~9.2)
registered the test before its data were seen. VGG-16 (BN) and the
basic-block ResNet of Table~\ref{tab:round3} were trained on CIFAR-10 under
the recipe of Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg} with no penalty, with the soft
orthogonality penalty SO ($\|WW^{\top} - I\|_F^2$, $\lambda = 10^{-4}$) and
with SRIP ($\lambda = 10^{-2}$), the strengths of Bansal \emph{et al.}
\cite{bansal2018orthogonality}, three seeds each; for every dense
convolution the measured $k^*(0.95)/r_{\max}$ of the penalised network is
compared with the \emph{ortho} counterfactual of the unpenalised network of
the same seed. Three outcomes were registered: P9.3, that the SO arm lands
within $0.1$ of the counterfactual in the median over layers; P9.4, that
SRIP does not (its minimisers need not be isometries
\cite{massart2022orthogonal}); and P9.5, that the width gained bears no monotone
relation to accuracy. Table~\ref{tab:ortho} gives the result.

P9.3 fails. Both penalties raise the level, from $0.35$--$0.36$ to
$0.53$--$0.56$ under SO and $0.42$--$0.54$ under SRIP, which is half of the
predicted gain (median fraction realised $0.50$ under SO, $0.24$ and $0.43$
under SRIP), leaving the SO arm $0.15$--$0.17$ below the counterfactual of
$0.71$--$0.73$. The shortfall has a visible cause. At these strengths the
penalties do not make the kernels isometric: their effective rank rises
from $0.53$--$0.56$ of $r_{\max}$ to $0.80$--$0.85$, and the counterfactual
recomputed from the penalised kernels themselves is still $0.72$--$0.76$
against their measured $0.42$--$0.56$, so the anisotropy that remains is
aligned against the input as it was before. The alternative the plan named,
that the penalty moves $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$, has no room at this threshold:
the patch covariance is full-rank at the $95\%$ criterion in every arm.
What the counterfactual does predict is the \emph{ordering} of the gains.
Across the layers of the SO networks the gain obtained correlates with the
gain predicted at $\rho = 0.72$ (ResNet, 41 layers) and $0.91$ (VGG-16, 8
layers); across the SRIP networks it does not ($-0.14$ and $-0.19$), which is
the form in which P9.4 holds. P9.5 holds as registered: over the twelve
penalised runs accuracy moves by $-0.1$ to $+0.3$ points, uncorrelated with
the width gained ($\rho = 0.15$). The counterfactual is therefore an upper
bound that a soft penalty approaches by about half, in the right layers; the
direct test, an exact isometry constraint such as ONI \cite{huang2020oni},
was not run.

\subsection{The Profile as a Diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

\emph{Exploratory; not pre-registered.} What is a profile for, once it is
calibrated? Two uses are within reach of the data already in hand, and
both are stated as readings, not results.

\emph{Where a trained ResNet-50 leaves width unused.} Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic} pools the six recipes: for every convolution it takes the median of $k^*(\tau)/r_{\max}$ over the recipes, then the median over the layers of one role in one stage. At $\tau = 0.999$, the criterion the truncation of Section~\ref{sec:projection} showed to be the width the classifier uses, 29 of the 53 layers are above $0.9$ in every recipe, and every $3\!\times\!3$ convolution of every stage is at $0.95$--$0.97$. The width that is not used sits at the entry of the network and in its projection shortcuts: the stem uses half of its 64-dimensional attainable rank ($0.49$; its input is a $7\!\times\!7$ RGB patch, Section~\ref{sec:rmax}), the first block of stage 1 uses $0.40$--$0.61$, and the four $1\!\times\!1$ stride-2 projection shortcuts use $0.61$, $0.72$, $0.85$ and $0.94$ from stage 1 to stage 4. These four layers and the stem are the only ones below $0.7$ in all six recipes. At $\tau = 0.95$ every role reads about one half; that number is the same in every stage and, by Section~\ref{sec:projection}, is not a width one can remove. The reading a designer takes from the table is therefore not ``ResNet-50 is half idle'' but ``its entry and its shortcuts are wider than what passes through them, by a factor of about two, in every recipe''.

\emph{The ruler's widths beside a pruning method's.} Reading $k^*(0.999)$ off the trained CIFAR-10 VGG-16 of Section~\ref{sec:learned} on $6{,}400$ training images gives, layer by layer, the widths $14, 47 \mid 107, 121 \mid 238, 247, 249 \mid 456, 376, 261 \mid 40, 40, 40$ (bars separate the five resolutions), $5.1$M parameters against $14.7$M. Network slimming \cite{liu2017slimming}, which learns per-layer widths by an $\ell_1$ penalty on the batch-norm scales and a global threshold, reports for its 16-convolution VGGNet on the same dataset (their supplementary Table~5) $22, 62 \mid 83, 119 \mid 193, 168, 85, 40 \mid 32, 32, 32, 32 \mid 32, 32, 32, 38$, at a test error below the unpruned network's. The two agree where the profile is decided: both cut the first convolution to a fifth or a third of 64, both keep the second and third resolutions nearly whole, and both collapse the last resolution to $32$--$40$ of $512$, the terminal collapse of Section~\ref{sec:learned} read by a different instrument. They disagree at the fourth resolution, where slimming removes $94\%$ and the ruler keeps $50$--$90\%$. The disagreement is informative about what each measures: the ruler reports the rank the trained network's activations actually span, slimming reports what can be removed when the network is retrained around the removal. Whether the ruler's widths retrain to the full network's accuracy is the pre-registered experiment of Section~\ref{sec:a18}: read at the convolution output they do on CIFAR-10 and not on CIFAR-100; read after the ReLU, the tensor the next layer consumes, they do at two thirds of the parameters; on ImageNet-1k the same reading names the full width of ResNet-18.

Three predictions were registered. P9.6, that the ruler's widths lose less than $0.3$ points on average and no seed falls more than $0.5$ below the full-width mean, holds: $93.55$ against $93.77$, a difference of $0.22$, with the lowest seed $0.40$ below. P9.8, that the $\tau = 0.95$ widths lose at least one point, holds with $2.15$ points: the training-side counterpart of Section~\ref{sec:projection}, where truncating a trained network to $k^*(0.95)$ costs it $19$--$21$ points, and the same statement about which threshold names the width in use. P9.7, that every uniform-cut seed falls below every ruler seed, was registered as a strict three-against-three ordering and does not hold as registered: eight of the nine pairs favour the ruler and the ninth is an exact tie, $9{,}337$ correct test images in both networks, so the pre-registered rule is failed by a single image. By the rule's own consequence we do not claim that the per-layer allocation is needed at this budget; the exploratory reading is a mean difference of $0.35$ points in the ruler's favour (uniform $93.20$), with the uniform arm's worst seed $0.46$ below the ruler's. The exploratory P9.9 also fails: the network retrained at the ruler's widths does not fill them ($0.85$ of $r_{\max}$ in the median, against $0.74$ for the full network, $0.92$ for the uniform cut and $0.98$ at the $\tau = 0.95$ widths), so a narrower network uses a larger fraction of its width without using all of it, and the count is re-made by training rather than inherited from the widths.

\emph{A second dataset (plan Section~9.7, registered after the CIFAR-10 result and before any CIFAR-100 data).} The same four arms on CIFAR-100, with the widths re-read from a CIFAR-100 full-width network (Table~\ref{tab:a18}, middle block), give a different answer. The ruler's widths are nearly the full ones in the last five layers ($466$--$490$ of $512$, against $40$ on CIFAR-10: the terminal collapse of Section~\ref{sec:learned} is the ten-class task's, and with a hundred classes the deep layers stay full), so the ruler removes only $13\%$ of the parameters, almost all of it from the first seven layers, whose widths it sets as on CIFAR-10. P9.14, the analogue of P9.6, fails: the ruler arm loses $0.59$ points ($72.64$ against $73.23$) and its lowest seed is $0.82$ below the full mean. P9.15 fails in the direction opposite to P9.7's tie: the uniform cut to the same parameter count is above the ruler arm in nine pairs of nine ($73.24$, indistinguishable from full width). P9.16 holds with $5.8$ points. The network retrained at the ruler's widths does fill them ($0.93$ of $r_{\max}$).

\emph{The tensor the next layer consumes (plan Section~9.8, registered after the CIFAR-100 result and before its data).} The failure has a candidate cause that the measurement itself exposes. The ruler reads the convolution output, before the nonlinearity, because that is where the bound of Section~\ref{sec:rmax} holds; but the layer after it consumes the post-activation tensor, and the ReLU raises the rank (Section~\ref{sec:blockout}). On both full-width networks the first convolution's output spans $14$--$15$ directions at $\tau = 0.999$ and its ReLU output $47$--$48$; the second, $47$--$48$ against $64$; from the third layer on the two agree within $5$--$25\%$ (CIFAR-10's last three layers excepted: $40$ against $168$--$347$). A layer narrowed to the rank of its own output therefore caps at that rank what the next layer receives, and the cap binds in the first two layers, whose input is a low-rank RGB patch. The plan registered a fifth arm, widths read after the ReLU ($47, 64 \mid 127, 128 \mid 255, 255, 254 \mid 504, 486, 469 \mid 347, 285, 168$ on CIFAR-10, $67\%$ of the parameters), with the prediction P9.17 that it retrains within $0.3$ of full width, and P9.18 that it beats the convolution-output widths seed for seed. Both hold: $93.84$ against $93.77$, every seed above every seed of the convolution-output arm (Table~\ref{tab:a18}). On CIFAR-100 the post-activation widths are $99\%$ of the full ones, so by the rule registered in advance that arm is not evidence for P9.17; it retrains to $73.49$.

What the three arms settle together is a statement about where the ruler is read. A width read from a trained network at $\tau = 0.999$ is the rank its activations span; read at the convolution output it is the width the layer \emph{produces}, and a network built at those widths loses accuracy where the nonlinearity had raised the rank before the next layer (CIFAR-100, $-0.59$); read after the nonlinearity it is the width the next layer \emph{consumes}, and a network built at those widths matches full width on CIFAR-10 at two thirds of the parameters. The measurement of this paper is taken at the convolution output because that is where $r_{\max}$ bounds it and where the kernel and the data separate (Section~\ref{sec:decompose}); a design decision must read the same profile one tensor later. The pre-registered claim that the convolution-output allocation beats a uniform cut is not supported on either dataset (a one-image tie, then a reversal); the post-activation allocation beats it on CIFAR-10 in nine pairs of nine, the only dataset on which it is a cut at all. A width read at $\tau = 0.95$ cannot be trained to on either dataset. The cost columns of Table~\ref{tab:a18} say what each cut buys: the post-activation widths remove a third of the parameters but, because the removed width sits in the low-resolution layers, only $12\%$ of the multiply-adds and $6\%$ of the inference time, whereas the uniform cut to the convolution-output arm's parameter count removes two thirds of the multiply-adds; the $\tau = 0.95$ widths run four to eight times faster at a cost of two and six points. None of this makes the ruler a pruning method: nothing is fine-tuned, the comparison is retraining from scratch, and one architecture and two datasets are one case.

\emph{The same arms in the representation (plan Section~9.9, registered after the results above and before any of its data).} Accuracy is one number per network; the plan asked whether the arms also agree with the full network layer by layer, by linear CKA \cite{kornblith2019cka} between globally pooled post-activation outputs on $6{,}400$ test images, with the two other full-width seeds as the ceiling that seed variation alone sets. The layer-median CKA to full-width seed 0 is $0.906 \pm 0.004$ at the ceiling, $0.910 \pm 0.002$ for the post-activation widths, $0.904 \pm 0.002$ for the convolution-output widths, $0.903 \pm 0.003$ for the uniform cut and $0.859 \pm 0.009$ at $\tau = 0.95$. The registered prediction (P9.19) had two parts: that the post-activation widths lie at least as close as the convolution-output widths, which holds in all three seeds, and that the two allocations differ most in the first three layers, which does not: the gap is $+0.07$ and $+0.09$ at the eleventh and twelfth layers, where the convolution-output ruler had set the width to $40$ and the post-activation ruler to $347$ and $285$. That is the distinction of the previous paragraph seen in the representation rather than in the accuracy: the network built at the convolution-output widths loses $0.22$ points, and its last layers are the ones whose representation has moved away from the full network's. Under corruption the same allocation is the one that costs (plan Section~9.9.4; CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019robustness}, $19$ corruptions at five severities, the same weights): mean corruption accuracy is $74.06 \pm 0.19$ at full width, $74.55 \pm 0.27$ at the post-activation widths (P9.24, within one point, holds, and the difference is in the narrower network's favour), $74.21 \pm 0.27$ at the convolution-output widths, so the registered P9.25, that the width removed there carries robustness, is not supported; the uniform cut to the same parameter count loses $1.66$ points, three times its clean loss, on blur, weather and digital corruptions alike. What the ruler's allocation buys over a uniform one is small in clean accuracy ($0.5$ points) and larger under shift. Transfer runs the other way (plan Section~9.9.2): a linear probe on the last pooled post-activation feature, whose dimension is the last layer's width ($512$, $168$, $40$, $298$ for full, post-activation, convolution-output and uniform), gives $22.8$ / $77.2$ / $41.3$ on CIFAR-100 / STL-10 / SVHN at full width, $21.4$ / $77.2$ / $39.0$ at the post-activation widths (P9.21, within one point on all three, not supported), $18.0$ / $76.6$ / $31.8$ at the convolution-output widths (P9.22, at least twice the clean loss on two of three sets, holds on all three) and $22.5$ / $76.1$ / $42.0$ for the uniform cut, which keeps the last layers wide. The width the ruler removes from the last layers is width the task does not use and a probe on another task does; the uniform cut keeps it and pays in clean and corrupted accuracy instead.

\emph{ImageNet-1k (plan Section~9.11, registered before any of its data).} The plan asked whether the post-activation result survives the scale, with ResNet-18 trained from scratch on ImageNet-1k under the \texttt{torchvision} reference recipe (SGD, batch 256, 90 epochs, step schedule, no distillation and no augmentation beyond the random resized crop and flip; the images pre-resized to a short side of 256, an implementation choice stated in the plan), two seeds per arm, the widths read after the ReLU on the seed-0 full network over $6{,}400$ training images at 16 positions, and, because the residual sum ties the block outputs of a stage to one width, each stage's output width set to the largest reading among its blocks. The ruler names the full width. Every site but the stem reads $0.98$--$1.00$ of its channels at $\tau = 0.999$; the stem reads $44$ of $64$, the low-rank RGB patch once more. The ruler arm therefore keeps $99.2\%$ of the parameters and $96\%$ of the multiply-adds, and the uniform cut matched to it removes $1.5\%$. The registered P9.29 holds by its letter ($69.00$ against $68.84$ top-1, both seeds within $0.35$ of the full mean) and P9.30 does not ($69.18$, $68.82$ against $69.09$, $68.58$, two of four pairs), but neither result carries weight: the three arms differ by less than the $0.60$ points between the two full-width seeds, and at this scale the experiment tests nothing about allocation, because the ruler found nothing to allocate. What it establishes is the reading. Read after the ReLU at $\tau = 0.999$, the ruler removes a third of VGG-16's parameters on CIFAR-10, one per cent on CIFAR-100 and one per cent of ResNet-18's on ImageNet-1k: the width a ten-class network leaves unused is the terminal collapse of Section~\ref{sec:learned}, and a network with a hundred or a thousand classes has no such margin after the nonlinearity. At $\tau = 0.95$ the same ImageNet network reads $0.64$--$0.87$ of its channels in the body, $0.34$ at the stem and $0.66$ at its last site, the thousand-class collapse visible only at the looser criterion; that is the margin which Section~\ref{sec:projection} shows a trained network cannot give up and which the $\tau = 0.95$ arms above show cannot be trained to (Table~\ref{tab:a18}, upper blocks).

\emph{Class count, controlled (plan Section~9.12, registered before any of its data).} The reading above ties the unused width to the number of classes across three datasets and two architectures, which differ in image count and resolution as well. The plan varied the class count alone: nested subsets of CIFAR-100 with $13$, $20$, $50$ and $100$ classes, the first $k$ of one fixed permutation with all $500$ images of each class ($13$ is the smallest subset whose $6{,}500$ images clear the $n/C \ge 50$ gate at the $2 \times 2$ layers; the ten-class point is the CIFAR-10 network above), VGG-16 under the same recipe with a $k$-way head, three seeds each, the post-activation widths read on each network's own training images; and a control that trains the ten-class CIFAR-10 task on $640$ images per class, the image count of the thirteen-class subset, to separate the class count from the image count that rises with it. Fig.~\ref{fig:classes} gives the outcome. The fraction of the parameters the ruler keeps rises monotonically with the class count in every seed, $0.88$, $0.92$, $0.98$, $0.99$ (P9.31 holds), and the last three layers, which the ten-class network collapses to $0.52$ of their width, go $0.83 \to 0.89 \to 0.98 \to 0.99$. The first seven layers do not move with the class count except the first, which follows the optimisation length instead ($54$ channels after $5{,}000$ steps, $47$ after $39{,}000$, and $43$ when the $6{,}400$ images are given the full $39{,}000$ steps), so P9.33 fails at that layer and holds at the other six. Two registered predictions fail. P9.32 asked the thirteen-class network to collapse below $0.6$ as the ten-class one does; it keeps $0.83$. P9.34 asked the ten-class collapse to persist, or deepen, on an eighth of the images, reasoning from neural collapse; the opposite happens: trained on $6{,}400$ images the ten-class network keeps $0.74$ of its last three layers and $0.83$ of its parameters, against $0.52$ and $0.67$ on the full set, so the collapse is reached through training and is not imposed by the class count alone. A follow-up registered after that result (plan Section~9.13) separates the images from the optimisation steps that rise with them: the same $6{,}400$ images trained for $780$ epochs, the $39{,}000$ steps of the full set, collapse the last three layers to $0.38$ and keep $0.62$ of the parameters, below the full set's $0.52$ and $0.67$ (P9.35 holds, three seeds $0.37$--$0.39$). The collapse is a matter of optimisation time, and at equal time fewer images deepen it, which is the direction P9.34 had reasoned from; what failed was the control, which had cut the step count along with the images. Because more images at a fixed epoch count deepen the collapse while more classes lift it, and the subset series raises both together, the monotone rise with the class count is not an artefact of the image count that accompanies it; if anything it is understated. What the control settles about the reading is this: the width a ten-class network leaves unused is the terminal collapse of Section~\ref{sec:learned} reached by a few-class network optimised long enough; it closes monotonically as classes are added and is gone by a hundred; and the last layer's rank at $\tau = 0.999$ sits far above the $C_{\rm class} - 1$ of neural collapse at every class count ($349$ directions for thirteen classes, $505$ for a hundred), so what the ruler sees closing is a heavy tail thinning, not the subspace theorem itself.

\subsection{Extension to a Transformer}
\label{sec:vit}

\emph{Exploratory; one checkpoint, no initialisation control.} Nothing in the protocol is specific to convolutions: a linear layer is a $1\!\times\!1$ convolution over its input vector, its attainable rank is $\min(d_{\rm in}, d_{\rm out})$, and over a token sequence the tokens play the role of positions. Table~\ref{tab:vit} applies the measurement to a ViT-B/16 \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} checkpoint (ImageNet-21k pre-training, ImageNet-1k fine-tuning) on the $6{,}400$-image subset with 32 tokens per image, so that the widest layer ($3{,}072$) clears the gate at $n/C = 67$. Two readings. The patch embedding, a $16\!\times\!16$ RGB patch mapped to $768$ dimensions, uses $0.07$ of its attainable rank at $\tau = 0.95$ and $0.22$ at $0.999$: like the ResNet stem, its input is low-rank and the bound is not what binds. And every one of the four linear roles rises with depth, from about a tenth of the attainable rank in the first blocks to one half or more in the last ($\rho$ against block index $0.76$--$0.99$), which is the monotone rise the pooled ImageNet profiles of Section~\ref{sec:related} report, here read at the layer, position-sampled, and against the attainable rank. Whether the rise is architectural or learned is the comparison with initialisation that this paper makes for convolutional families and does not make here; the residual stream and the attention map are not hooked. The extension is in the released package.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Sources of the Published Disagreement}
\label{sec:why}

Section~\ref{sec:intro} set out to reconcile a profile that saturates and
collapses \cite{garg2019lowEffort} with one that rises
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim}. The reconciliation is not the one we expected.
The estimator (Section~\ref{sec:estimator}) does not change the ordering of layers, only
the level, by more than the profile's own step, so the two literatures' numbers are not on one scale. The summary
statistic does change the ordering (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}): the participation ratio and the ruler
disagree layer by layer, and on a plain network they run in opposite directions; but the direction
is the reverse of the one that would reconcile the two published shapes, so the statistic explains part of the disagreement and not its sign. The tensor is: a profile read at block
outputs is higher by the expansion ratio at every bottleneck expansion
(Section~\ref{sec:blockout}), and the denominator adds a sawtooth to any
profile read at convolution outputs (Section~\ref{sec:rmax}). And the
terminal collapse is a property of the task: measured with one protocol, our
own CIFAR-10 VGG-16 collapses in its last three layers and our ImageNet
VGG-16 does not (Section~\ref{sec:learned}), and the controlled variation of
Section~\ref{sec:a18} ties the collapse to the class count and to the
optimisation length. The two published profiles
differ, then, in what tensor they read and what task they were trained on,
and would differ in level even if they agreed on both.

\subsection{Reporting Requirements}
\label{sec:protocol}

The three pitfalls of Sections~\ref{sec:rmax}, \ref{sec:estimator} and \ref{sec:blockout}, and the two further requirements below, are not errors in any published work. Each is a choice
that a measurement must make, and each was made differently by different
studies without the consequence being known. Stated as a protocol, a
reported effective-width profile should say: which tensor is read
(convolution output, post-activation, or block output), because the rank
bound binds the first and not the third and the two differ by the expansion
ratio; whether spatial positions or images are the observations, because
the pooled value is lower at essentially every layer by more than the
profile's own step; what the denominator is, because the nominal width is
unattainable at every expansion layer and a profile divided by it
alternates with the architecture rather than with the representation;
that depthwise and dense layers are on separate axes; and the
sample-to-channel ratio at the widest layer, because under pooling it is
the image count and fails at $2048$ channels for any dataset under
$10^5$ images. None of this is expensive. The whole first measurement took about five hours on one consumer GPU, and the second ran on a laptop.

\subsection{Implications for Choosing Width}

The descriptive question was how much of its width a trained layer uses. Once the denominator is right the answer has two parts. At the $95\%$ criterion a trained dense convolution uses between $0.33$ and $0.72$ of its attainable rank in the median over layers, across eleven families; that number orders layers and tracks training but is not a count of removable width, since truncating to it costs ResNet-50 $21$ points (Section~\ref{sec:projection}). At the $99.9\%$ criterion it uses $0.84$--$0.93$ of its attainable rank, and in six of eight checkpoints truncating to that costs nothing a paired test detects. For those networks the second number is the one a width-selection method has to explain: a seventh of the attainable rank, and a third of the nominal width, of a conventionally trained ResNet-50 carries no variance its classifier uses. The two RSB-trained checkpoints show that this margin is a property of the recipe: the same architecture trained with binary cross-entropy and heavy augmentation uses about two points' worth of the variance beyond $k^*(0.999)$. It is still not a pruning ratio (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), because a rank restriction on a trained network is not a narrower network trained from scratch; and Section~\ref{sec:a18} shows what the count is good for and where it must be read: a network built at the convolution-output $k^*(0.999)$ widths and trained from scratch matches full width on CIFAR-10 at a third of the parameters but loses $0.6$ points on CIFAR-100, and one built at the post-activation $k^*(0.999)$ widths, the tensor the next layer consumes, matches full width at two thirds of the parameters with every seed above the convolution-output arm; the $k^*(0.95)$ widths lose two and six points. On ImageNet-1k the post-activation ruler names the full width of ResNet-18 to within one per cent, the stem excepted, so there is nothing there to allocate; the margin the ruler finds on CIFAR-10 is the few-class collapse, it closes monotonically as classes are added (Fig.~\ref{fig:classes}) and opens only with enough training, and a thousand-class network does not have it to give.

What differs across architectures is how much of the profile is learned.
For plain and basic-residual networks the ordering of layers is the
architecture's and training adds a level; a method that sets widths from
the architecture alone, or from the network at initialisation, would get
the ordering right for those families. For the ImageNet ResNet-50
checkpoints it would not: their trained ordering is uncorrelated with the
initial one and the depth trend has the opposite sign, so any width chosen
before training would be chosen against a profile the network will not
have. The controlled experiment of Section~\ref{sec:learned} says that
this is not the bottleneck block's doing, since under one CIFAR-10 recipe
the bottleneck network keeps as much of its initial ordering as the basic
one; what a width chosen at initialisation is worth therefore depends on
the training regime, not on the block, and the regime that produces the
ImageNet reordering is not identified here. The trajectories add that the
profile settles well before accuracy does in all four architectures
measured, and that the ImageNet-like reversal of the depth trend appears
in every CIFAR-10 network in its first epoch and is undone.

What a width criterion should target follows from the same numbers. A criterion that reads $k^*(0.95)$ as ``the width in use'' would remove directions the classifier depends on (Section~\ref{sec:projection}); the functional count is nearer $k^*(0.999)$.
A criterion must divide by the attainable rank, or it will report every
expansion layer as three-quarters idle. And whether the count can be raised
by regularisation is not a matter of pushing the kernel toward an isometry
alone: Section~\ref{sec:decompose} shows the trained kernel's high-gain
directions are aligned with the input's high-variance ones
($\rho_{\rm align} \approx +0.9$), so the
prediction for orthogonalisation is a rise of about $0.3$ of $r_{\max}$,
not a jump to the ceiling, and the penalties actually used realise half of
that rise, in the layers the decomposition names, because they leave the
kernel well short of an isometry. A width-selection method built on this
statistic is a separate piece of work; what this paper fixes is the
quantity it should optimise and the checks it must pass.

The same protocol measures depth as naturally as width, and we have used
it that way only where a question forced us to: the block-level profile
of Section~\ref{sec:blockout}, the depth recursion of the data factor in
Section~\ref{sec:decompose}, and the reversal of the depth trend under
training in Section~\ref{sec:learned}. How much effective width each
block \emph{adds}, and which blocks a trained network could do without
\cite{veit2016ensembles}, is a different measurement with its own
controls, and we leave it to a study of its own.

\subsection{Scope of the Claims}

Everything here is a linear measure of the channel covariance at one
tensor: not the intrinsic dimension of a manifold, not the rank of the
network's Jacobian, and not the spatial rank of a feature map. A layer whose
covariance has high rank may still carry a low-dimensional curved manifold,
and the gap is not small (Section~\ref{sec:four}). Nor is any measurement
here, by itself, a statement that a network built with fewer channels would
train to the same accuracy; where the paper makes that statement
(Section~\ref{sec:a18}) it does so by retraining, for one architecture, and
it holds for the post-activation widths at $\tau = 0.999$ on a few-class
task and nowhere else. The measurement is of the trained artefact, not of
the optimisation that produced it.

Two further things the paper does not say. It does not say that the
published profiles were wrong: each is a correct measurement of the tensor,
estimator and denominator it chose, and the disagreement between them is
at least partly a disagreement about those choices rather than about the
networks. And it does not say what the shape of the profile is. The
pre-registered shape hypotheses were tested with a rule that had no power,
the paired replacements find level differences and not shape differences,
and the two shape statements we make, the reversal of the depth trend in
ResNet-50 and the terminal collapse of few-class networks, rest on six
recipes and five seeds and on three seeds per class count, the first
labelled exploratory and the second reported against its registered rule.

\subsection{Failure Cases}
\label{sec:fails}

A measurement is worth what its failures are worth. Twelve places in this paper are ones where the protocol says nothing, where what it says is inside its own noise, or where a prediction registered in advance was wrong; they are collected here so that the boundary is in one place.

\emph{Where it is silent.} (i)~The pooled estimator cannot be read at a wide layer: with $n$ the image count, no dataset under $10^5$ images estimates a $2048$-channel layer at $n/C \ge 50$, eleven of ResNet-50's pooled rows fail our own gate, and Section~\ref{sec:estimator} declines to compare estimators there. (ii)~The bound $r_{\max}$ is necessary and not sufficient: the stems of VGG-16 and the MobileNets reach only $0.3$--$0.4$ of it at $\tau = 0.999$ because the RGB patch is itself low-rank (Section~\ref{sec:rmax}), so at a first layer the ratio measures the data and the bound is not what binds; on ImageNet-1k the ruler cuts the ResNet-18 stem and nothing else (Section~\ref{sec:a18}). (iii)~Corollary~\ref{cor:kstar} is empty when the kernel is ill-conditioned: at the trained median $\kappa = 10.6$ its bounds at $\tau = 0.95$ are $k^*(0.14)$ and $k^*(0.9995)$ of the counterfactual spectrum, which is why Section~\ref{sec:decompose} reports the information content of the bound per layer rather than assuming it.

\emph{Where the effect is inside the noise.} (iv)~The pooling level shift, which exceeds the profile's own adjacent-layer step in $18$ of $19$ networks, does not in DenseNet-121, whose step is the structural $1\!\times\!1$/$3\!\times\!3$ alternation and twice the size of the shift (Table~\ref{tab:pooling}). (v)~Trained-versus-untrained for ConvNeXt-T, once its depthwise and dense layers are kept apart as Section~\ref{sec:dwsplit} requires, is $p = 0.013$ in one checkpoint and $p = 0.21$ in the other; the first-round test that pooled the two kinds and gave $p = 6 \times 10^{-4}$ is withdrawn. (vi)~MobileNetV3-Large's random draws agree with one another only at $0.75$ ($0.37$ for the depthwise layers), so for that family the initialisation control is itself unstable and the trained-versus-random comparison has no fixed reference (Section~\ref{sec:learned}). (vii)~The profile is not a property of the network alone: on white noise the trained ResNet-50 profile keeps $\rho = 0.32$ of its ordering and $1/f$ noise recovers $0.70$ (Section~\ref{sec:robust}), so a profile has to state its image set, and Section~\ref{sec:protocol} does.

\emph{Where a registered prediction failed.} (viii)~H9.1, that the block type is what makes the ImageNet ResNet-50 recipes reorder their layers, fails under a controlled experiment (basic and bottleneck blocks agree with their initialisations equally, T--R $0.55$ against $0.50$); the cause is in the data or the recipes and this study could not afford the experiment that separates them (Table~\ref{tab:round3}). (ix)~P9.3, that orthogonality penalties reach the isometric counterfactual of Proposition~\ref{prop:ostrowski}, fails by half: SO realises $0.50$ of the predicted gain and SRIP $0.24$--$0.43$, with kernel effective ranks of $0.80$--$0.85$ that are far from isometric (Table~\ref{tab:ortho}); the prediction of \emph{which} layers gain holds for SO and not for SRIP. (x)~$k^*(0.999)$ bounds the width in use for conventional recipes and not for the RSB recipes, which place function in directions holding under $0.1\%$ of the variance and lose $1.7$ points when those are removed (Table~\ref{tab:projection}); the threshold that names the width in use is recipe-dependent. (xi)~The ruler read at the convolution output is not a design width: its CIFAR-100 arm loses $0.59$ points and the uniform cut to the same parameter count beats it in nine pairs of nine (Table~\ref{tab:a18}); and on CIFAR-10, where its accuracy is within $0.22$ of full width, the CKA of its last two layers, which it had set to $40$ channels, is $0.07$--$0.09$ below the post-activation arm's, so a width that is sufficient for the accuracy is not thereby sufficient for the representation, and the two should not be conflated. Read one tensor later, after the ReLU, the ruler passes both tests on CIFAR-10 and names the full width on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1k, where the pre-registered ImageNet experiment (P9.29, P9.30) therefore tests nothing about allocation: what it measures is that the unused width at $\tau = 0.999$ is the few-class task's. (xii)~Of the class-count control's four registered predictions three fail, one of them at a single layer: the first convolution's width follows the optimisation length where the registered prediction had it fixed, as the next six are; the thirteen-class subset keeps $0.83$ of its last three layers' width, not the $0.6$ registered by analogy with the ten-class network, and a ten-class network trained on an eighth of its images keeps $0.74$ rather than the full set's $0.52$, because the control had cut the optimisation steps by the same factor as the images; at the full step count the same images collapse to $0.38$ (P9.35), so the registered reasoning was right about the images and the control was wrong about what it varied (Fig.~\ref{fig:classes}). Each of these is reported at the place cited, with the registered rule and its outcome; none is softened by an unregistered re-analysis.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Six, in rough order of how much they should temper the conclusions.

\emph{Error bars exist for ResNet-50 and for family-level statements only.}
Six recipes and five seeds put a between-checkpoint spread beside every
ResNet-50 number. The other families have one to six trained checkpoints and three seeds, enough for the ordering statistics of
Table~\ref{tab:families} and not for any statement about one of their
layers; VGG-16's two checkpoints are with and without batch normalisation,
so its recipe agreement compares two architectures. The VGG-16 trajectory
is one seed; the controlled experiment and the penalty test have three
seeds per arm, enough for their registered tests and not for layer-level
statements.

\emph{The pooled estimator is sample-limited for wide layers, ours included.}
Under pooling $n$ is the image count, so no dataset smaller than $10^5$ images
can estimate a $2048$-channel layer at $n/C \ge 50$. Eleven of ResNet-50's
pooled rows fail our own gate, and we decline to compare estimators there. This
is a limit on the published pooled measurements as much as on ours.

\emph{Everything here is linear.} $k^*$, PR, effective rank and stable rank are
all functionals of the channel-covariance spectrum. None estimates the
dimension of a manifold, and the gap is not small: Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} report a PC-based dimension of order $200$ and a
TwoNN intrinsic dimension of order $18$ for the same VGG-16 layer. A layer
whose covariance has high rank may still carry a low-dimensional curved
manifold.

\emph{Not a claim about prunability.} That the directions beyond $k^*(0.999)$ can be truncated at inference without a loss the test resolves (Section~\ref{sec:projection}) does not by itself establish that a network built with fewer channels would train to the same accuracy; Section~\ref{sec:a18} tests that by retraining, for one architecture, and finds it true of the post-activation widths on CIFAR-10 and empty elsewhere. The measurement is of
the trained artefact, not of the optimisation that produced it, and the
literature on lottery tickets and on over-parameterised optimisation gives
ample reason to keep those separate.

\emph{Scope.} ImageNet classification, with detection backbones only in an ablation and a transformer only in one exploratory profile (Section~\ref{sec:vit}), without the initialisation control that carries the convolutional comparisons. The other datasets enter through training experiments: CIFAR-10 through the
validation gate, the trajectories, the controlled block experiment, the
penalty test and the retraining arms, CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1k through the
retraining arms alone (Section~\ref{sec:a18}, one architecture each), and the class-count control of Section~\ref{sec:a18} varies the class count at one head depth and finds that the optimisation length matters as well, so the comparison between the two CIFAR datasets (Section~\ref{sec:learned}) is confounded with head depth and with training length. The
controlled experiment shows that the block is not what makes the ImageNet
ResNet-50 recipes reorder their layers, but it varies the block at CIFAR-10
scale ($32 \times 32$ inputs, ten classes, 100 epochs) and so cannot say
whether the ImageNet effect is the dataset's or the recipes'; the
experiment that would, the same three architectures under one ImageNet
recipe with several seeds, is the one this study could not afford. Head
depth at fixed class count was not varied, so the terminal collapse
reported on CIFAR-10 is explained here only in part: it needs few classes
and long optimisation, and whether the head's depth also enters is open. Arguments about the first convolutional
layer are additionally bounded by the dimension of the input patch and
confounded by the observation of Bell and Sejnowski \cite{bell1997ica} that
such layers learn filters resembling independent rather than principal
components.

\emph{The inverted-residual finding is about available checkpoints.} The MobileNetV3 checkpoints differ in pre-training data and training length as well as in optimiser, and the five same-dataset EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints, which agree only moderately, span three training frameworks. The controlled MobileNetV2 (Table~\ref{tab:round3}) shows that the block does have a profile under one recipe, so the weak agreement is the recipes'; which ingredient of the recipes is not determined.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to reconcile two published depth profiles of how much of its
width a convolutional layer uses. Three choices that the profile literature
makes silently each move the profile by more than the differences it
reports: normalising by nominal rather than attainable width caps every
expansion layer at the expansion ratio and misreads bottleneck networks as
underused, in all 14 networks that have such layers; estimating the covariance after global pooling lowers the value at 687 of 703 layers, and by more than the profile's own step in 18 of 19 networks; and reading the block output instead of
the convolution output raises it by the expansion ratio in 161 of 161
blocks. With those choices fixed and stated, the protocol reproduces the one published CIFAR-10 table it can be compared with ($r = 0.98$, two layers differing by $0.2$), and what training changes differs across the families measured: trained networks largely keep the ordering the architecture gave them and gain a level, the profile is in place before the fit is, and a controlled experiment registered in advance finds that the block type does not alter this; the ImageNet ResNet-50 checkpoints, which do reorder their layers, and the inverted-residual checkpoints, which do not agree with one another, are both the work of their data or their recipes rather than of their blocks. Measurement code, the eigenvalue
spectra of every layer, and the analysis plan with its deviation log are
released so that the next profile can be compared with these on stated
terms.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:ostrowski}.} From the singular value
decomposition $W = U S V^{\top}$ (thin, $S$ of size $r \times r$),
$P = U S U^{\top}$ and $W_{\circ} = U V^{\top}$ satisfy $P W_{\circ} = W$, so
$\Sigma_{\rm out} = W \Sigma_{\rm patch} W^{\top} = P \Sigma_{\circ} P$.
Both $\Sigma_{\rm out}$ and $\Sigma_{\circ}$ are supported on the column
space of $U$, on which $P$ is invertible with eigenvalues
$\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r$. Restricted to that space, Ostrowski's theorem
\cite[Thm.~4.5.9]{horn2013matrix} for the congruence $P \Sigma_{\circ} P$
gives $\lambda_i(\Sigma_{\rm out}) = \theta_i \lambda_i(\Sigma_{\circ})$ with
$\lambda_{\min}(P^2) \le \theta_i \le \lambda_{\max}(P^2)$, i.e.\
$\theta_i \in [\sigma_{\min}^2, \sigma_{\max}^2]$. \qed

\emph{Corollary~\ref{cor:kstar}.} Let $F_{\rm out}(k)$ and $F_{\circ}(k)$
be the fractions of total variance in the top $k$ eigenvalues of the two
spectra. From $\sigma_{\min}^2 \lambda_i(\Sigma_{\circ}) \le
\lambda_i(\Sigma_{\rm out}) \le \sigma_{\max}^2 \lambda_i(\Sigma_{\circ})$,
\[
  F_{\rm out}(k) \;\ge\; \frac{F_{\circ}(k)}{F_{\circ}(k) + \kappa^2 \bigl(1 - F_{\circ}(k)\bigr)},
\]
since the top-$k$ sum is scaled by at least $\sigma_{\min}^2$ and the tail by at most $\sigma_{\max}^2$. The right-hand side is at least $\tau$ exactly when
$F_{\circ}(k) \ge \kappa^2 \tau / (1 - \tau + \kappa^2 \tau) = \tau'$. Hence
the smallest $k$ with $F_{\circ}(k) \ge \tau'$ satisfies
$F_{\rm out}(k) \ge \tau$, which is the upper bound. The same two inequalities read the other way give $F_{\rm out}(k) \le \kappa^2 F_{\circ}(k) / (\kappa^2 F_{\circ}(k) + 1 - F_{\circ}(k))$, which is below $\tau$ exactly when $F_{\circ}(k) < \tau''$; hence every $k$ below $k^*(\tau''; \Sigma_{\circ})$ has $F_{\rm out}(k) < \tau$, which is the lower bound. For $\kappa = 1$ the
two spectra are proportional and the counts coincide; if also
$C_{\rm out} \ge d$ then $W_{\circ}$ has orthonormal columns, so
$\Sigma_{\circ}$ has the nonzero spectrum of $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$ truncated to
$r_{\max} = d$ dimensions. \qed

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:gate}.} Lawley's first-order expansion is $\mathbb{E}\,l_i = \lambda_i + (\lambda_i/n)\sum_{j \ne i} \lambda_j/(\lambda_i - \lambda_j) + O(n^{-2})$, and the trace of the sample covariance is unbiased. Summing over $i \le k$, the terms with both indices at most $k$ cancel in pairs ($\lambda_i\lambda_j/(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)$ is antisymmetric), leaving the double sum over $i \le k < j$, each term of which is nonnegative. Under $\lambda_j \le \lambda_i/2$ for $j > k$, $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i - \lambda_j) \le 2$, so each term is at most $2\lambda_j$ and the sum is at most $2k \sum_{j > k}\lambda_j = 2k\,(1 - F(k))\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$. \qed

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:truncation}.} $\mathbb{E}\|r\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\bigl((I - U_kU_k^{\top})\Sigma_\ell\bigr) = \sum_{i > k}\lambda_i$. The first-order change of the logits is $J_\ell r$, whose second moment is $\operatorname{tr}(J_\ell\,\mathbb{E}[rr^{\top}]\,J_\ell^{\top}) = \operatorname{tr}(J_\ell \Sigma_\ell^{\perp} J_\ell^{\top}) \le \|J_\ell\|_{\rm op}^2 \operatorname{tr}\Sigma_\ell^{\perp}$. For several layers the perturbations add and Minkowski's inequality bounds the root mean square of the sum by the sum of the root mean squares. \qed

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:compression}.} Let $Q$ ($r \times d$) have
orthonormal rows spanning the row space of $W$; then
$W_{\circ} = U Q$ for some $U$ with orthonormal columns, and
$\Sigma_{\circ} = U (Q \Sigma_{\rm patch} Q^{\top}) U^{\top}$ is unitarily similar
to the compression $Q \Sigma_{\rm patch} Q^{\top}$. The Cauchy interlacing
theorem \cite[Thm.~4.3.28]{horn2013matrix} gives the inequalities, with
equality of the nonzero spectra when $Q$ is square. Since $Q$ depends only
on the row space, so does $\Sigma_{\circ}$. \qed

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}.} The patch vector is
$p = [a_1; \ldots; a_{k_h k_w}]$ with each $a_j$ almost surely in
$\mathcal{R} = \operatorname{range} \Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$, so $p$ lies
almost surely in $\mathcal{R}^{k_h k_w}$, whose dimension is
$k_h k_w \operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$; the covariance of a
random vector supported on a subspace has rank at most the subspace's
dimension. The second inequality follows from
$\operatorname{rank}(W \Sigma W^{\top}) \le \min(\operatorname{rank} W,
\operatorname{rank} \Sigma)$ and $\operatorname{rank} W \le C_{\ell + 1}$. \qed

\subsection*{Limiting Cases}

Each proposition has a regime in which it says nothing, and the regime is worth naming.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:gate}, no spectral gap.} The expansion is termwise in $1/(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)$ and requires $\lambda_k > \lambda_{k+1}$; as the gap at $k$ closes the bound loses its content. The statistic does not: $\hat F(k)$ remains biased upward, but $k^*(\tau)$ is then determined only to within the number of eigenvalues clustered at the cut, and that is the residual seen in the convergence data, where the layers whose $k^*(0.999)$ at $n/C = 50$ has not reached its final value differ from it by one component.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:truncation}, $\tau \to 1$ and $\tau \to 0$.} The bound vanishes linearly in $1 - \tau$ and is trivial at $\tau = 0$, where it returns the whole energy; between, it is a first-order statement, and for a ReLU network the second-order term is not controlled, so the proposition fixes the scale of the perturbation and not the accuracy. The multi-layer bound is a triangle inequality and is attained only when the per-layer perturbations of the logits are collinear.

\emph{Corollary~\ref{cor:kstar}, $\kappa \to \infty$.} As $\kappa$ grows $\tau' \to 1$ and $\tau'' \to 0$, so the two bounds become $1$ and $r$ and the interval is empty of information. The interval is informative only while $\kappa^2 (1 - \tau) \ll \tau$: at the median condition number of the trained kernels, $\kappa = 10.6$, and $\tau = 0.999$, the bounds are $k^*(0.90)$ and $k^*(0.99999)$ of the counterfactual spectrum, and at $\tau = 0.95$ they are $k^*(0.14)$ and $k^*(0.9995)$. This is why Section~\ref{sec:decompose} reports the bound's information content per layer rather than assuming it.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:compression}, $r < d$.} When the kernel's row space is a proper subspace of the patch space the interlacing is strict in general; the upper bound is attained if and only if the row space contains the top-$r$ eigenvectors of $\Sigma_{\rm patch}$, the lower if and only if it is spanned by the bottom $r$. For $r < d$ the counterfactual therefore depends on \emph{which} $r$ directions of the input the kernel keeps, a choice that training makes and the polar factor does not undo; the propositions bound its effect by interlacing and do not determine it. The case $r = d$, where nothing is chosen, is the $1\!\times\!1$ expansion, $C_{\rm out} \ge d$.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}, saturation.} The bound $k_h k_w \operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm act}^{(\ell)}$ exceeds $d$ as soon as the previous layer's post-activation covariance is full-rank, and it then says only $\operatorname{rank} \Sigma_{\rm patch}^{(\ell+1)} \le d$, which is Equation~\eqref{eq:rmax}; it binds only after a layer whose activation rank has collapsed, as in the CIFAR-10 tail of Section~\ref{sec:learned}.

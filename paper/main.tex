{Li: Measuring the Effective Width of Convolutional Layers}

\begin{abstract}
How much of a convolutional layer's channel width does a trained network
use? Two published depth profiles of this quantity disagree, and we show that
much of the disagreement is manufactured by the measurement rather than by
the networks. Measuring the channel covariance of every convolutional layer in
22 public ImageNet checkpoints spanning seven architecture families, we
isolate three pitfalls. First, a $1\!\times\!1$ expansion layer cannot have
output rank above its input width; a known bound that the measurement
literature has never carried into its denominator. Normalising by attainable
rather than nominal width removes an apparent two-population structure in
every one of the 14 models that contain such layers. Second, estimating the
covariance from globally pooled features lowers the measured dimensionality in
687 of 703 dense layers, with the remaining 16 being integer ties. Third, the
output of a residual block exceeds the bound that binds its last convolution in
161 of 161 bottleneck blocks, so profiles taken at block outputs and at
convolution outputs are different objects. With these fixed, the protocol
recovers the published CIFAR-10 profile of Garg \emph{et al.} on a network we
train ourselves ($r = 0.98$ over 13 layers). We then ask what training changes,
using two to six checkpoints and three to five random initialisations per
family on one image set. The answer depends on the block type. In plain,
basic-residual and dense networks the layer ordering is set by the
architecture and training raises the level. In bottleneck ResNet-50 the
ordering is learned: six training recipes agree with each other
($\rho = 0.84$) and not with initialisation ($\rho = -0.08$), and the depth
trend reverses sign. In inverted-residual networks no recipe-independent
profile exists. Code, spectra and the pre-registered analysis plan with its
deviation log are released.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Convolutional networks, effective dimensionality, channel covariance,
network width, representation analysis.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} number of channels in each layer of a convolutional
network is, in almost all practice, either inherited from a published
architecture or found by search. Neural architecture search returns a width
for a stated compute budget at a cost that can reach \num{$10^3$} GPU-hours
\cite{cai2020ofa}; structured pruning returns a smaller network, but only
after being \emph{given} a target ratio \cite{fang2023depgraph}. There is
reason to think per-layer width is the variable that matters: Frankle
\emph{et al.} \cite{frankle2021missingMark} showed that the per-weight
decisions of pruning-at-initialisation methods can be replaced by a per-layer
keep fraction without loss, and Liu \emph{et al.}
\cite{liu2019rethinkingPruning} found that the pruned architecture, not the
inherited weights, carries the benefit of pruning.

A prior question is descriptive: of the width a trained network \emph{has},
how much does it use? Concretely, how many directions in a layer's channel
space carry its activation variance, as a fraction of the directions
available? The question has been asked twice at scale, with different
answers. Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort} computed, for each
convolutional layer of VGG networks on CIFAR, the number of principal
components of the channel-space activation covariance needed to reach a fixed
fraction of the variance, and reported a profile that saturates in the middle
of the network and collapses in its last layers. Elmoznino and Bonner
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim} computed the participation ratio across
\num{500}\,+ convolutional layers of ImageNet-trained models and reported
effective dimensionality \emph{increasing} with depth.

Setting out to reconcile these, we found that the two measurements differ in
ways that are not about the networks at all, and that the same choices
silently shape any new measurement one might make. Three of them matter.

\emph{The denominator.} A $1\!\times\!1$ convolution that expands $C_{\rm in}$
channels to $C_{\rm out} > C_{\rm in}$ cannot produce an output of rank above
$C_{\rm in}$. The bound is elementary and has been stated for convolutional
expansion layers before \cite{han2021rexnet,kim2018rank1}, but no depth
profile in the measurement literature divides by it. Twenty of ResNet-50's 53
convolutions are such layers, so a profile normalised by nominal width is
architecturally capped at $0.25$ at every fourth layer, and reads as
``wide layers use a tenth of their width''.

\emph{The estimator.} Garg \emph{et al.} treat spatial positions as samples;
Elmoznino and Bonner average each feature map to one vector per image before
estimating the covariance. The two covariances are different objects, and
they had not been compared on the same layers.

\emph{The measurement point.} Some studies hook the output of a convolution;
others hook the output of a residual block, after the shortcut has been added.
The rank bound above binds the former and not the latter.

This paper does two things. It fixes a measurement protocol in which each of
these choices is made explicitly and its consequence quantified, and it then
uses the protocol to ask what training actually changes about a layer's
effective width. The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A protocol, validated.} We measure the channel covariance of
  the convolution output (before activation and before any shortcut) from
  sampled spatial positions, at a sample-to-channel ratio of at least $50$,
  normalised by attainable rank, with depthwise and dense convolutions kept
  apart. On a VGG-16 we train ourselves on CIFAR-10 the protocol recovers the
  13-layer profile published by Garg \emph{et al.} \cite{garg2019lowEffort}
  with $r = 0.98$ and a mean absolute difference of $0.05$; two layers differ
  by $0.2$, and we say which (Section~\ref{sec:repro-garg}).

  \item \textbf{Three pitfalls, quantified across 22 checkpoints.} Normalising
  by attainable rank removes the bounded-versus-unbounded difference in all 14
  models that contain expansion layers, for expansion ratios of 2, 4 and 6.
  The pooled estimator gives a lower value than the position-sampled one in
  687 of 703 dense layers and 79 of 80 depthwise layers, the exceptions being
  integer ties at $C = 32$. The output of a bottleneck block exceeds the rank
  bound of its last convolution in 161 of 161 blocks, by a factor of about
  four (Sections~\ref{sec:rmax}, \ref{sec:estimator} and \ref{sec:blockout}).

  \item \textbf{Training acts differently on different block types.} With two
  to six trained checkpoints and three to five random initialisations per
  family, measured on the same images, we compare the ordering of layers in
  the trained profile with the ordering at initialisation. In plain,
  basic-residual and densely connected networks (VGG-16, ResNet-18/34,
  DenseNet-121) the ordering is largely architectural
  ($\rho_{\rm trained,random} = 0.6$--$0.75$) and training raises the level by
  $0.09$--$0.19$. In bottleneck ResNet-50 the ordering is learned: six recipes
  agree with each other ($\rho = 0.84$), agree with initialisation not at all
  ($\rho = -0.08$, against a random-versus-random ceiling of $0.94$), and the
  trend with depth reverses from falling to rising. In inverted-residual
  networks (MobileNetV3, EfficientNet-B0) checkpoints do not share a profile,
  the level of the $1\!\times\!1$ layers varies four-fold across recipes, and
  the depthwise layers halve their effective width on training
  (Section~\ref{sec:learned}).

  \item \textbf{Two exploratory observations.} Along one CIFAR-10 training
  run of VGG-16 the profile is in place by epoch 40, while test accuracy
  still rises from $84\%$ to $94\%$. And the position-sampled statistic
  computed from $6{,}400$ images agrees with the $50{,}000$-image value to
  within $0.016$ on every layer of six checkpoints, which bounds how much
  data such a measurement needs (Section~\ref{sec:samplesize}).
\end{itemize}

The analysis was specified before the ImageNet data were collected. The
pre-registered hypotheses concerned the \emph{shape} of the profile, and
none of them survived a paired test across all layers; the third contribution
above was arrived at afterwards and is labelled exploratory wherever it
appears. The plan, and a log of every departure from it, is released with the
code (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Four notions of ``dimensionality''}
\label{sec:four}

At least four distinct quantities are described in this literature as the
dimensionality of a representation, and they are not interchangeable. The
first is the intrinsic dimension of the data manifold, estimated by nonlinear
methods such as the two-nearest-neighbour estimator of Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic}. The second is the rank of the linear span of the
representation. The third is the participation ratio of the covariance
spectrum. The fourth is the number of principal components required to reach a
chosen fraction of the total variance.

The gap between the first and the rest is not a matter of degree. For the same
hidden layer of the same VGG-16 network, Ansuini \emph{et al.} report a
manifold dimension of approximately \num{18} against approximately \num{200}
principal components at a $90\%$ variance criterion---one to two orders of
magnitude apart. They state explicitly that their findings cannot be recovered
by linear estimates such as PCA.

Everything measured in this paper is linear: every quantity we report is a
function of the eigenvalues of a channel-space covariance matrix. We make no
claim about manifold dimension, and where prior results are cited we name the
quantity rather than the word.

\subsection{Depth profiles of representation dimensionality}
\label{sec:profiles}

Several groups have reported how a dimensionality measure varies with depth,
and the reported shape depends on the measure. Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} found that manifold intrinsic dimension rises in
early layers, peaks at a relative depth of roughly \num{0.2--0.4}, and then
decreases monotonically---a shape that Recanatesi \emph{et al.}
\cite{recanatesi2019dimensionality} also observed, and that Rangamani
\emph{et al.} \cite{rangamani2023intermediate} recovered again using stable
rank on intermediate layers.

The deep end of any such profile is partly determined analytically. Neural
collapse \cite{papyan2020neuralCollapse} implies that the penultimate-layer
features of a converged classifier concentrate on a subspace of dimension
$C_{\text{class}}-1$, independent of that layer's nominal width. For CIFAR-10
this is $9$; for ImageNet-1k it is $999$. A profile measured on a ten-class
problem is therefore forced toward a terminal collapse that a thousand-class
problem need not exhibit, and the two regimes cannot be compared without
saying so. We exploit this: our validation experiment runs on CIFAR-10 and
our main experiment on ImageNet, so both regimes are available by design.

One nearby result should not be confused with these. Feng \emph{et al.}
\cite{feng2022rankDiminishing} report a rank that decreases monotonically with
depth, but the object measured there is the numerical rank of the network's
Jacobian---a property of the map, not of the activation covariance. Similarly,
the rank used by HRank \cite{lin2020hrank} is the singular-value rank of an
individual $H\times W$ feature map for one channel and one image, which is a
spatial quantity and not the channel covariance spectrum.

The bound we use in Section~\ref{sec:stats} to normalise by attainable rather
than nominal width is not new. That a layer's output rank cannot exceed its
input dimension was made explicit for the softmax layer by Yang \emph{et al.}
\cite{yang2018softmax}, for attention heads whose size is below the sequence
length by Bhojanapalli \emph{et al.} \cite{bhojanapalli2020lowRank}, and for
convolutional expansion layers by Han \emph{et al.} \cite{han2021rexnet},
who plot the rank of $1\!\times\!1$, $3\!\times\!3$ and inverted-bottleneck
outputs against $C_{\text{in}}/C_{\text{out}}$ in random-weight networks and
turn the observation into a design rule (an expansion ratio of at most six).
Kim \emph{et al.} \cite{kim2018rank1} write the standard-convolution form
$\min(C_{\text{in}} k_h k_w,\, C_{\text{out}})$ in an im2col derivation.
What none of these do, and what the measurement literature above has not done
either, is carry the bound into the denominator of a depth profile measured on
trained networks. Section~\ref{sec:rmax} shows that doing so removes an
apparent two-population structure in ResNet-50.

\subsection{Where the measurement is taken, and what is learned}
\label{sec:where}

The studies above do not hook the same tensor. Garg \emph{et al.}
\cite{garg2019lowEffort} and NORTH\textsuperscript{*} \cite{maile2022north}
read the output of a convolution; Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} and Elmoznino and Bonner
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim} read the output of a residual block, after the
shortcut has been added and the nonlinearity applied. In a bottleneck block
the last convolution is a $1\!\times\!1$ expansion whose output rank is
bounded by its input width; the shortcut is not, so the block output can, and
in Section~\ref{sec:blockout} does, exceed that bound. Han \emph{et al.}
\cite{han2021rexnet} make the complementary point for random networks: the
nonlinearity after an expansion layer raises the rank above the bound again.
Neither choice of tensor is wrong, but a depth profile is a different object
depending on which was taken, and the published profiles do not say which
comparisons across them are meaningful.

A second question the profile literature leaves open is how much of a
reported profile is a property of the trained network rather than of the
architecture and the input statistics. Elmoznino and Bonner
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim} note that ``architecture-specific factors can
affect [effective dimensionality] in ways that are independent of learning''
and do not pursue it; Han \emph{et al.} \cite{han2021rexnet} measure rank in
random-weight networks only. Saxe \emph{et al.} \cite{saxe2014exact} show,
for deep linear networks, that the singular structure of the representation
is set by the input--output covariance and the initialisation together, which
makes the comparison of a trained profile with its own initialisation the
natural control. We are not aware of a published depth profile that reports
it. Section~\ref{sec:learned} does so for seven architecture families, with
several trained checkpoints and several random initialisations each, and
finds that the answer depends on the block type.

\subsection{Spectral decay, and why a Marchenko--Pastur threshold is unusable}
\label{sec:mp}

A natural way to decide how many components are ``real'' is to threshold the
spectrum at a noise floor derived from random matrix theory. That route is not
available here.

The eigenspectra of convolutional representations are approximately power-law:
Ghosh \emph{et al.} \cite{ghosh2022powerLaws} fit
$\lambda_i \propto i^{-\alpha}$ to the empirical feature covariance of
intermediate layers and relate $\alpha$ to downstream generalisation, and Kong
\emph{et al.} \cite{kong2022v1Eigenspectrum}, fitting over a stated index
range, find $\alpha$ substantially below unity for conventionally trained
networks. A power law with decay that slow has no bulk edge separating a
signal regime from a noise regime, so a Marchenko--Pastur threshold has nothing
to cut at. We therefore use variance-fraction and entropy-based statistics
throughout and report their threshold sensitivity, rather than claiming a
principled noise floor we cannot justify.

Two cautions attach to this literature. Martin and Mahoney
\cite{martin2021implicitSelfReg} established heavy-tailed spectra for
\emph{weight} matrices; that result concerns a different object and does not
transfer to activations, which is one reason we measure the latter directly.
And Pospisil and Pillow \cite{pospisil2025revisiting} argue that the
comparable spectrum in visual cortex is better described as a broken power law
than a single one, and identify three estimator biases---sampling noise in the
covariance, the fact that $n$ observations yield at most $n$ nonzero
eigenvalues, and bias in cross-validated PCA estimators. All three apply to the
present setting, and Section~\ref{sec:samplesize} addresses them.

\subsection{Redundancy, pruning, and the selection of width}

That trained convolutional networks carry substantial redundancy is well
established. Suau \emph{et al.} \cite{suau2020filterDistillation} compress
layers by analysing the correlation structure of filter responses; Waleffe and
Rekatsinas \cite{waleffe2023pcn} exploit low-rank activation structure that is
present early in training; Nanda \emph{et al.}
\cite{nanda2023diffusedRedundancy} show the redundancy is diffuse, in that a
random subset of neurons in a pretrained layer suffices for downstream tasks.
None of these reports a normalised depth profile.

A separate line grows networks rather than shrinking them, beginning with
cascade-correlation \cite{fahlman1990cascade} and continuing through splitting
steepest descent \cite{liu2019splitting}, GradMax \cite{evci2022gradmax},
NORTH\textsuperscript{*} \cite{maile2022north} and self-expanding networks
\cite{mitchell2024selfExpanding}. Every one of them ultimately requires a
tuned quantity---a target size, a fixed per-phase growth fraction, a threshold
on a growth score, or an external resource budget. Because that observation is
the subject of a companion study, we do not develop it here.

One of these methods requires specific comment, because it uses a statistic of
the same family as ours. NORTH\textsuperscript{*} \cite{maile2022north}
triggers growth on the $\epsilon$-numerical rank of a layer's post-activation
matrix, normalised by that layer's width, and applies this to convolutional
filters in VGG-11 and WideResNet-28 on CIFAR. The statistic is therefore not
novel to this work, and we say so again in Section~\ref{sec:models}. What that
work does not do---and what this paper contributes---is report the quantity as
a profile across depth, compare it against the alternative summary statistics
and estimators in use, measure it at ImageNet scale on pretrained checkpoints,
or contrast it against an untrained control. We also note their own conclusion
that a maximum layer size remains necessary in their setting to prevent
unbounded growth without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The measured object}
\label{sec:object}

Let a convolutional layer produce an output tensor
$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C \times H \times W}$ over a batch of $N$ images.
Writing $y_{n,:,h,w} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ for the vector of channel responses at
one spatial position of one image, the object of interest is the $C \times C$
covariance of that vector.

Two estimators of it appear in the literature, and they are not the same
object. The \emph{position-sampled} estimator draws $p$ spatial positions
uniformly at random from each image and treats each as one observation, giving
$n = Np$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Sigma}^{\text{pos}}
  = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{n,(h,w) \in \mathcal{P}_n}
    \bigl(y_{n,:,h,w}-\bar{y}\bigr)\bigl(y_{n,:,h,w}-\bar{y}\bigr)^{\!\top}.
  \label{eq:covpos}
\end{equation}
The \emph{globally-pooled} estimator first averages each feature map over its
spatial extent and treats each image as one observation, giving $n = N$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Sigma}^{\text{pool}}
  = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n}
    \bigl(\tilde{y}_n-\bar{\tilde{y}}\bigr)
    \bigl(\tilde{y}_n-\bar{\tilde{y}}\bigr)^{\!\top},
  \quad
  \tilde{y}_n = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} y_{n,:,h,w}.
  \label{eq:covpool}
\end{equation}
Pooling removes the within-image spatial variance rather than summarising it,
so the two estimators can order layers differently. Garg \emph{et al.}
\cite{garg2019lowEffort} used a spatial estimator and Elmoznino and Bonner
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim} a pooled one. The latter report, in their
supplementary S6 Text, that their conclusions about encoding performance
survive when dimensionality is instead computed on the entire flattened
feature map; but that object is $CHW$-dimensional with images as
observations---neither Equation~\eqref{eq:covpos} nor
Equation~\eqref{eq:covpool}---and the dimensionality values under the two
treatments are not reported. To our knowledge the pooled and
position-sampled channel covariances have not been compared on the same
layers. We compute both.

Equation~\eqref{eq:covpool} also has a consequence that matters for
Section~\ref{sec:samplesize}: under pooling, $n$ is the number of
\emph{images}, so a layer with $C=2048$ observed over $10^4$ images is
estimated at $n/C \approx 5$.

\subsection{Summary statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

Let $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_C \ge 0$ be the eigenvalues of the
estimated covariance. We report:
\begin{align}
  k^*(\tau) &= \min\Bigl\{k : \textstyle\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i
               \big/ \sum_i \lambda_i \ \ge\ \tau \Bigr\},
               \label{eq:kstar}\\
  \mathrm{PR} &= \Bigl(\textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\Bigr)^{\!2}
                 \Big/ \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i^2 ,
               \label{eq:pr}\\
  \mathrm{erank} &= \exp\Bigl(-\textstyle\sum_i p_i \log p_i\Bigr),
                    \quad p_i = \lambda_i \big/ \textstyle\sum_j \lambda_j ,
               \label{eq:erank}
\end{align}
together with the stable rank $\sum_i \lambda_i / \lambda_1$ and the exponent
$\alpha$ of a least-squares fit of $\log \lambda_i$ against $\log i$ over a
stated index range. Equation~\eqref{eq:kstar} is the statistic of
\cite{garg2019lowEffort} and Equation~\eqref{eq:pr} that of
\cite{elmoznino2024highDim}; Equation~\eqref{eq:erank} is the effective rank of
Roy and Vetterli \cite{roy2007effectiveRank}, which like PR requires no
threshold but, unlike PR, responds to the tail of the spectrum. Each is
reported both raw and divided by the layer's nominal channel count $C$, since
the question is what fraction of the available width is used.

A layer's output at one spatial position is a linear map of its
receptive-field patch, so the rank of $\hat{\Sigma}$ is bounded above by the
rank of that map and not by $C$ alone
\cite{kim2018rank1,han2021rexnet}. The bound holds at the point we record
the convolution output---before any nonlinearity and before a residual
addition; a subsequent ReLU or skip connection can raise the rank above it
\cite{han2021rexnet}. For a convolution with $C_{\text{in}}$
input channels, kernel $k_h \times k_w$ and $g$ groups, the weight matrix is
block-diagonal with blocks of disjoint support, so the block ranks add and
\begin{equation}
  r_{\max} = g \cdot \min\!\Bigl(
     \tfrac{C_{\text{in}}}{g} k_h k_w,\ \tfrac{C_{\text{out}}}{g}
  \Bigr).
  \label{eq:rmax}
\end{equation}
Where $r_{\max} < C$, the ratio $k^*/C$ is bounded above by $r_{\max}/C$
whatever the network has learned, and normalising by $C$ therefore charges the
layer for width it never had. The case is not hypothetical: a bottleneck's
$1\!\times\!1$ expansion has $C_{\text{in}} = C_{\text{out}}/4$, so $k^*/C$
there cannot exceed $0.25$ by construction. We report $k^*/C$, which is the
pre-registered statistic and the one comparable with prior work, and
$k^*/r_{\max}$ alongside it. Section~\ref{sec:rmax} shows what the choice of
denominator does.

Following the pre-specified analysis plan, $k^*(0.95)/C$ is the primary
outcome and all other statistics are secondary. We note that
\cite{garg2019lowEffort} used $\tau = 0.999$, a looser criterion that is more
exposed to the tail of the spectrum and hence to sampling bias; we use that
value only when comparing directly against their published table.

\subsection{Estimation}

Covariances are accumulated in a single pass using the pairwise update of Chan
\emph{et al.} \cite{chan1983variance}, with the running mean and scatter held
in double precision. This is not a formality. Accumulating uncentred outer
products in single precision loses several significant digits, and the first
casualties are the small eigenvalues---which are exactly what $k^*(\tau)$
depends on for $\tau$ near one. The same update lets partial results computed
on separate machines be combined exactly.

The choice of $p$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:covpos} requires comment. Setting
$p = HW$, that is, using every spatial position, maximises the nominal sample
count but not the information: activations at adjacent positions are strongly
correlated, so $NHW$ overstates the number of independent observations. Since
the sample-size analysis of Section~\ref{sec:samplesize} turns on that count,
using all positions would flatter it. We therefore subsample positions and
report $n = Np$ as the sample size, which is conservative.

We measure VGG-16 with batch normalisation, the architecture used by
\cite{garg2019lowEffort}; ResNet-18 and ResNet-50 \cite{he2016resnet}, which
that work excluded; and ConvNeXt-T \cite{liu2022convnext}, which postdates it.
The four span three connectivity patterns and two normalisation regimes. All
use publicly released \texttt{torchvision} ImageNet-1k checkpoints, and all
layers are probed on the full ImageNet validation split of $50{,}000$ images
\cite{deng2009imagenet}.

Convolutional and post-activation outputs are recorded separately. A
rectified representation is non-negative, which changes the covariance
structure, so combining the two in one profile would confound them. Depthwise
convolutions are flagged, since a depthwise layer does not mix channels and its
channel covariance therefore carries a different meaning; this matters for
ConvNeXt, which is predominantly depthwise.

As a control we measure ResNet-50 and ConvNeXt-T again at initialisation,
without training; these are the deepest and the most recent of the four, and
the two whose profiles are least like VGG's. This separates structure the network acquired from structure implied
by the architecture together with the second-order statistics of natural
images \cite{field1987relations}. We note in passing that any argument about
the first convolutional layer is bounded by the dimension of the input patch
itself, and is further complicated by the observation of Bell and Sejnowski
\cite{bell1997ica} that the filters such a layer learns resemble independent
components rather than principal ones.

As stated in Section~\ref{sec:related}, the normalised-rank statistic is not
introduced here; NORTH\textsuperscript{*} \cite{maile2022north} uses a member
of the same family as a growth trigger. Our contribution is the profile, the
comparison of estimators and statistics, and the controls.

\section{Sample-Size Control}
\label{sec:samplesize}

Every number in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a function of an estimated
covariance, so the first question is not what the profile looks like but at
what sample size it stops moving. We answer that before showing any profile.

Two facts bound the exercise. A sample covariance formed from $n < C$
observations is rank-deficient by construction, so its $k^*$ is a statement
about $n$ and not about the network. Well above that point the small
eigenvalues remain downward-biased, and $k^*(\tau)$ for $\tau$ near one depends
on exactly those. The analysis plan therefore fixed, before the data existed, a
reporting rule: no row with $n/C < 50$ is reported, and every reported
statistic must additionally be shown to have flattened in $n/C$.

Under the position-sampled estimator of Equation~\eqref{eq:covpos} with
$p = 16$ positions per image over $50{,}000$ images, each convolutional layer
receives $n = 8 \times 10^5$ observations. This gives $n/C$ between $391$
(ResNet-50's $2048$-channel layers) and $12{,}500$ ($64$-channel layers), so
every convolutional row in the study clears the gate with at least a factor of
seven in hand. Fig.~\ref{fig:conv} shows the convergence itself: metrics are
recomputed at $n/C \in \{1,2,5,10,25,50,100,250\}$ within the same single pass,
so the control costs no extra compute. Of the layers measured, none is still
moving by more than $0.02$ between the last two checkpoints in five of the six
runs; VGG-16 has one layer of thirteen that is, and it is excluded.

The pooled estimator of Equation~\eqref{eq:covpool} is in a different position,
and this is not a detail. Under pooling $n$ is the number of \emph{images}, so
$n = 5 \times 10^4$ and $n/C$ runs from $781$ down to $24.4$. For VGG-16,
ResNet-18 and ConvNeXt-T every pooled row clears the gate; for ResNet-50,
eleven of fifty-three do not, and they are precisely the $1024$- and
$2048$-channel layers. Any comparison of the two estimators on those layers is
therefore confounded with sample size, and we do not make one. This limit
applies to the published pooled measurements as well as to ours: a
$2048$-channel layer cannot be estimated at $n/C \ge 50$ from any dataset with
fewer than $10^5$ images.

\subsection{The normalised profile}
\label{sec:profile}

Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} gives $k^*(0.95)/C$ against relative depth for all four
architectures. The first observation is the one that survives every
qualification that follows: no layer of any trained network in this study
requires more than $0.78$ of its nominal width to account for $95\%$ of its
channel-space variance, and the per-architecture medians are $0.42$ (VGG-16),
$0.51$ (ResNet-18), $0.25$ (ResNet-50) and $0.66$ (ConvNeXt-T). Section
\ref{sec:rmax} shows that the ResNet-50 figure is largely a property of its
denominator.

Shape is decided by the pre-registered rule rather than by inspection:
Spearman $\rho$ of the statistic against depth order over all gated layers
($\rho_{\text{all}}$) and over the last third ($\rho_{\text{late}}$), with
$\rho_{\text{late}} < -0.5$ read as a late collapse and
$\rho_{\text{all}} > 0.5$ with $\rho_{\text{late}} > 0$ as a monotonic rise.
Table~\ref{tab:shape} reports both. VGG-16 rises strongly
($\rho_{\text{all}} = +0.813$) and then turns over
($\rho_{\text{late}} = -0.800$); ConvNeXt-T shows the same qualitative pattern;
neither ResNet meets either criterion.

That verdict needs a magnitude beside it, and the reason is worth stating
plainly because it decides how this result relates to the prior literature.
Spearman $\rho$ is rank-based and therefore scale-free: a curve slipping from
$0.53$ to $0.44$ across its last three layers earns exactly the same
$\rho_{\text{late}} = -0.800$ as one falling from $0.97$ to $0.07$. We
therefore report, as a descriptive addition that was \emph{not}
pre-registered, the drop across the last third in the units of the statistic
itself. VGG-16's is $0.059$. ConvNeXt-T's is $0.246$. The terminal collapse
reported by \cite{garg2019lowEffort} is $0.90$. The pre-registered test and
the magnitude say different things about VGG-16, and both belong in the record:
the curve does turn over, and the turn is an order of magnitude smaller than
the effect it is being compared against.

\subsection{Normalising by attainable rank}
\label{sec:rmax}

\emph{Exploratory.} ResNet-50 is the outlier of Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} on every
reading: the lowest median, no shape by the pre-registered test, and a profile
that alternates violently between adjacent layers. Grouping its layers by
nominal width rather than by depth makes the alternation legible---the median
$k^*(0.95)/C$ runs $0.23, 0.34, 0.40, 0.37$ for $C = 64 \ldots 512$ and then
falls to $0.11$ and $0.13$ for $C = 1024$ and $2048$---and invites the reading
that the widest layers use roughly a tenth of the width they are given.

That reading is an artefact of the denominator. Twenty of ResNet-50's
fifty-three convolutional layers are the $1\!\times\!1$ expansions at the end
of each bottleneck, for which $C_{\text{in}} = C_{\text{out}}/4$ and hence
$r_{\max}/C = 0.25$ by Equation~\eqref{eq:rmax}. Those layers cannot exceed
$0.25$ however well they are trained, and at $\tau = 0.999$ they reach a median
of $93\%$ of that ceiling (range $45$--$98\%$ over the seventeen of them, with
the two lowest values both in the first bottleneck of the network). What looked
like a tenth of the available width is most of the rank the architecture
permits. Against $r_{\max}$ the width profile becomes
$0.23, 0.34, 0.42, 0.42, 0.43, 0.47$: flat, slightly rising, and with no
collapse at the wide end. The median moves from $k^*/C = 0.250$ to
$k^*/r_{\max} = 0.422$, in line with VGG-16 ($0.422$) and ResNet-18 ($0.533$).
ResNet-50 is not an outlier; it was being asked the wrong question.

The bound itself is known (Section~\ref{sec:profiles}); Han \emph{et al.}
\cite{han2021rexnet} observed in random-weight networks that ``drastic
channel expansion harms the rank''. What is added here is that in a trained
network the expansion layers sit at $93\%$ of the bound, so the effect they
describe is, for ResNet-50, accounted for by the bound alone, and that a
profile which does not divide by it misreads the architecture as underused
width.

The correction is checkable rather than merely plausible: $k^*(0.999)/r_{\max}$
must not exceed unity, and across all six runs its maximum is $0.973$ to
$0.992$. The effect is confined to bottleneck architectures---one bounded layer
of thirteen in VGG-16, three of twenty in ResNet-18, one of twenty-two in
ConvNeXt-T, against twenty of fifty-three in ResNet-50---which is why it can
go unnoticed in a study of VGG alone, and why it matters for a literature that
increasingly measures residual networks.

\subsection{Do the summary statistics agree?}
\label{sec:metrics}

The pre-registered comparison is whether $k^*(0.95)/C$ and $\mathrm{PR}/C$
assign the same shape to identical layers. They do, on five of the six runs.
The one exception is ConvNeXt-T at initialisation, where $k^*$ reads a
monotonic rise and PR no clear shape; on the trained models the two agree
everywhere. H1 is not supported: the choice between these two statistics does
not explain the disagreement in the literature.

\subsection{Does the estimator explain the disagreement?}
\label{sec:estimator}

It does. Computing both estimators of Section~\ref{sec:object} on the same
forward pass, over the same layers, with the same spectral machinery, the
shape verdict changes with the estimator on four of the six runs.

VGG-16 is the cleanest case, and it is also the architecture on which the two
prior results were obtained. Position-sampled, its profile rises to $0.527$ and
turns over, giving $\rho_{\text{all}} = +0.813$, $\rho_{\text{late}} = -0.800$
and a verdict of late collapse. Globally pooled, over the same thirteen layers,
it runs from $0.047$ to $0.328$ with $\rho_{\text{all}} = +0.962$ and
$\rho_{\text{late}} = +1.000$: a monotonic rise, without exception, to the last
layer measured. All thirteen pooled rows clear the sample-size gate, so the
difference is not a sampling artefact. ResNet-18 behaves the same way, with
twenty of twenty pooled rows gated.

Two things follow. Pooling lowers the level everywhere---it averages away the
within-image spatial variance, which is carried by many channels---and it
removes the terminal turnover. Since \cite{garg2019lowEffort} used a spatial
estimator and \cite{elmoznino2024highDim} a pooled one, and since to our
knowledge the two have never been computed on the same layers, the most
economical account of the disagreement between those literatures is that they
measure different objects. H2 is supported.

We do not extend this to ResNet-50: eleven of its pooled rows fail the gate
(Section~\ref{sec:samplesize}), and they are the layers where the difference
would be largest.

\subsection{Trained versus untrained}
\label{sec:trained}

The profiles are not implied by architecture and input statistics alone.
Against its untrained control, trained ConvNeXt-T is higher on seventeen of
twenty-two layers (mean difference $+0.085$) and trained ResNet-50 on
forty-four of fifty-three (mean $+0.108$, maximum $0.469$). The separation is
structured rather than uniform: ConvNeXt-T's trained and untrained curves are
nearly coincident from mid-depth onwards and diverge in the early layers, while
ResNet-50's diverge progressively with depth, its untrained profile declining
($\rho_{\text{all}} = -0.540$) where its trained one does not.

The control also settles which of the exploratory findings are about learning.
ConvNeXt-T's late drop of $0.246$ is absent at initialisation, where the same
layers give $-0.038$: that turnover is learned. ResNet-50's decline of
$k^*/C$ with width, by contrast, is present at initialisation and stronger
there ($\rho(C) = -0.897$ untrained against $-0.378$ trained), consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:rmax}---it is the rank bound, which training cannot move.

\subsection{Threshold sensitivity}
\label{sec:threshold}

$k^*(\tau)$ needs a threshold, and \cite{garg2019lowEffort} used
$\tau = 0.999$, four orders of magnitude looser in retained variance than the
$0.95$ used here and correspondingly more exposed to the biased tail of the
spectrum. Fig.~\ref{fig:tau} varies $\tau$ over $\{0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999\}$ on
VGG-16. The level moves a great deal---the last layer reads $0.29$, $0.44$,
$0.74$ and $0.95$---but the shape does not: all four curves rise steeply to
relative depth $0.5$, dip at the same layer, recover, and turn over in the last
two. H4 is not supported. The threshold sets how much of the tail is counted,
not whether the profile has a hump.

\subsection{Depthwise and dense convolutions}
\label{sec:dwsplit}

\emph{Exploratory.} Section~\ref{sec:models} records depthwise layers
separately on the ground that a layer which does not mix channels has a
channel covariance of a different kind. ConvNeXt-T makes that distinction
consequential: eighteen of its twenty-two convolutional layers are depthwise
and four are dense, and the two groups do not overlap. The depthwise layers
have median $k^*(0.95)/C = 0.707$ and a late drop of $0.246$; the dense ones
median $0.309$ and a rise. Interleaved along a single depth axis they produce
the sawtooth visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:profile}, and the aggregate shape verdict
is in part an artefact of that interleaving. We report the stratified numbers
and the aggregate; a profile of ConvNeXt-style architectures that does not
separate them is measuring two things at once.

\subsection{Limitations}
Five, in rough order of how much they should temper the conclusions.

\emph{One checkpoint per architecture.} We measure the publicly released
weights, so there is no seed variance anywhere in this study and no error bar
on any profile. Differences between architectures are large relative to the
effects we claim, but differences between two layers of one network may not be.

\emph{The pooled estimator is sample-limited for wide layers, ours included.}
Under pooling $n$ is the image count, so no dataset smaller than $10^5$ images
can estimate a $2048$-channel layer at $n/C \ge 50$. Eleven of ResNet-50's
pooled rows fail our own gate, and we decline to compare estimators there. This
is a limit on the published pooled measurements as much as on ours.

\emph{Everything here is linear.} $k^*$, PR, effective rank and stable rank are
all functionals of the channel-covariance spectrum. None estimates the
dimension of a manifold, and the gap is not small: Ansuini \emph{et al.}
\cite{ansuini2019intrinsic} report a PC-based dimension of order $200$ and a
TwoNN intrinsic dimension of order $18$ for the same VGG-16 layer. A layer
whose covariance has high rank may still carry a low-dimensional curved
manifold.

\emph{Not a claim about prunability.} That a trained network does not use most
of a layer's channel-space variance does not establish that a network built
with fewer channels would train to the same accuracy. The measurement is of
the trained artefact, not of the optimisation that produced it, and the
literature on lottery tickets and on over-parameterised optimisation gives
ample reason to keep those separate.

\emph{Scope.} ImageNet classification only: no detection, no segmentation, no
transformer, and no dataset whose class count differs---which is exactly the
gap Section~\ref{sec:classcount} exists to close. Arguments about the first
convolutional layer are additionally bounded by the dimension of the input
patch and confounded by the observation of Bell and Sejnowski
\cite{bell1997ica} that such layers learn filters resembling independent rather
than principal components.
